How to Read a Canadian Course Calendar Like a Chair

You have decided you want to teach a course. So you go looking for a Canadian course calendar, click through four levels of a department website, and end up staring at either nothing useful or two hundred course listings with no signal about which ones need a body next term.

So you send the email everyone sends: "I have 20 years of experience in finance and would be interested in teaching in your program."

I have read a lot of those emails. They do not fail because the experience is unimpressive. They fail because the chair reading them has a Tuesday and Thursday 6pm section of one specific course code to fill, and your file asks them to figure out which of forty courses you meant. That is homework, not a solution.

The short version: before you contact anyone, open the Canadian course calendar for one institution and pick three specific courses by code. One core course you could teach next Monday, one adjacent course that needs preparation, and one stretch course that needs real build time. Name the codes, the department and the campus. That is a file a chair can act on in thirty seconds.

Why named codes beat "open to anything" is covered in the three-course rule for adjunct teaching in Canada. This post is the document work that rule assumes: how to find the right calendar, how to tell it apart from the timetable and the outline, how to pick a core, an adjacent and a stretch, and how to defend each one from your actual work.

What a Chair Actually Does With Your File

Here is the thirty seconds you are competing for.

A chair staffing a term is working from a list of unstaffed sections. Course code, section number, day, time, campus. When a file arrives naming three courses that exist in the calendar, the chair reads it against that list. Match or no match, decision made, file kept or filed.

When a file arrives saying "I could teach finance," the chair has to assign meaning to it before it becomes useful. Which finance course. What level. Diploma or degree.

Can this person handle a first-year service section of 90 students taught to a common outline, or are they picturing a fourth-year seminar in their specialty? That interpretation work is real work, and it happens in a week where the chair is also handling scheduling conflicts, room changes and a faculty member on leave.

Naming the course is not a clever tactic. It is the difference between handing someone a solution and handing them a project.

The Order of Operations Nobody Outside the Building Sees

Per-course teaching gets assigned in a sequence. Understanding the sequence is what makes the three-course method obviously correct rather than a tip.

First, full-time faculty load gets built. Those obligations are contractual and they get satisfied before anything else moves.

Second, collective agreement rights are honoured. Published agreements in the sector typically contain load-assignment and right-of-first-refusal style provisions for existing contract academic staff. Pull the agreement for the institution you are targeting and you will see the priority structure spelled out. Existing sessional and partial-load instructors with standing generally get first look at courses they have already taught.

Third, whatever sections are left over get staffed from whoever is available and credible.

Your unsolicited file mostly matters at stage three. That is not a reason to be discouraged. It is a reason to be precise, because at stage three the chair is not evaluating potential. They are matching people to codes under time pressure.

Why Canadian Departments Are Leaning on Per-Course Hiring Right Now

On January 22, 2024, IRCC announced a two-year cap on international student permit applications, with roughly 360,000 approved study permits expected for 2024, a decrease of about 35 percent from 2023. In September 2024, IRCC announced a further reduction to the 2025 target. The reported figure was revised more than once, so pull the current release yourself rather than trusting a number in a blog post.

Stack that on top of Ontario's domestic tuition environment. The province cut domestic tuition by 10 percent in 2019-20 and held it frozen for several subsequent years, with limited increases permitted more recently. Check the current framework directive before you quote specifics.

Two revenue lines squeezed at once. Across 2024 and 2025, Ontario public colleges responded with program suspensions and staff reductions. Sheridan, Centennial, Mohawk, Algonquin, St. Lawrence and Conestoga were among the institutions reported to have suspended programs. OPSEU and an arbitrated faculty contract put the figure at more than 600 programs cancelled or suspended. I am deliberately not quoting an aggregate total of my own, because the reported numbers moved repeatedly through the year and most of the figures circulating online are stale.

Here is the part that matters for you. Budget pressure does not eliminate sections. It eliminates full-time lines. The courses still have to run, and the flexible, per-course option becomes more attractive to a department that cannot commit to a permanent hire.

For scale: Universities Canada represents close to 100 Canadian universities, and Colleges and Institutes Canada represents more than 120 publicly supported colleges, institutes, cégeps and polytechnics. Both counts shift, so confirm them against the current membership lists. That is the size of the market you are actually looking at.

Do You Need a PhD to Teach College in Canada? Not for These Courses

This is the myth that stops most industry people before they start.

Credential requirements in Canada are set course by course and institution by institution. College diploma-level teaching and university service or professional courses routinely accept a relevant graduate credential, or a professional designation plus substantial practice. For the pathway question behind that mapping, see how to get a university teaching job with a CPA or P.Eng. and no PhD.

The strongest verifiable proof point sits in AACSB's business accreditation standards. Accredited business schools formally recognize four faculty qualification categories: Scholarly Academic, Practice Academic, Scholarly Practitioner and Instructional Practitioner. The Instructional Practitioner category exists explicitly for faculty qualified by sustained professional experience and current practice rather than a doctorate. Read the current AACSB business accreditation standards rather than repeating figures secondhand.

Read that again. Accredited business schools have a designed slot for you. It is not a loophole, it is a category in the standards.

So can a CPA teach an introductory tax or audit course without an accounting master's? Can a P.Eng teach engineering economics without a graduate degree? In a lot of college and service-course settings, yes. The judgment is made at the course level.

The gap between HR and the chair

Here is the part that confuses smart candidates. A posting may list credential language that appears to exclude you, and you may still get hired for a specific section.

That is because HR runs a credential screen against a posting, while the chair makes a qualification judgment about a named course and documents the rationale. "This candidate has 15 years of controllership experience and a CPA, and the course is a first-year financial accounting section taught to a common outline" is a defensible file note. "This candidate has industry experience" is not.

Which is why naming the course matters more than pre-clearing your credentials. You are giving the chair the specific thing they have to justify.

How to Read a Canadian Course Calendar With Hiring Eyes

Most candidates cannot find the right document, then conclude the information does not exist. Three different documents, three different jobs.

Academic calendar vs timetable vs course outline

The academic calendar is the catalogue. It is the authoritative list of course codes, titles, credit weights, descriptions, prerequisites and antirequisites, and institutions generally treat it as their public commitment to students. This is where your three courses come from. Every course code you name should be copied out of a live calendar entry.

The timetable is the term-by-term schedule of when sections actually run. This is your demand signal, and almost nobody uses it.

The course outline or syllabus is the learning-outcome document. This is the real job description, and I will come back to it.

If you have been calling the timetable a "course calendar," that is why you could never find anything. The three-course rule post covers how to count sections from the timetable. Below is how to use all three documents together.

Decoding course codes and numbers

At universities, numbering usually runs by level. 100-level is first year, 200-level second year, 300 and 400-level upper undergraduate, and 500 and above is typically graduate. Conventions vary by institution, and colleges use their own, so read the description and the program year rather than assuming the number means the same thing everywhere.

If you are coming from industry, your target is the low end. First and second-year service courses are where sessional demand tends to concentrate. The fourth-year seminar in your specialty is the course a tenured professor is protecting, not the one sitting unstaffed in August.

Use the timetable to count sections

Open the current timetable and count how many sections of a course actually run. One section per year is a course that gets covered internally. Nine sections across three terms, several of them in the evening, is a course with turnover and a chair who has needed help before.

Section count is the single best public demand signal you have. Use it.

One note on college calendars

Ontario college programs are built against provincially approved program standards with published vocational learning outcomes. Check the ministry's program standards page for your target program.

Where those documents exist, the course description tells you close to exactly what the department needs demonstrated, which makes defending a claim easier than at a university, where calendar entries are looser and you need the outline to find the real content.

The Three-Tier Structure: Core, Adjacent, Stretch

Three courses. Not one, not ten. Here is the logic from the chair's side.

Name one course and you are useful only if that exact gap opens. Name ten and the chair has to audit your credibility across ten claims, which is more work than the hire is worth. Three covers a realistic spread of gaps and stays auditable in under a minute. That is an editorial judgment from sitting on the other side of the table, not a researched statistic.

The core course. Highest volume, most defensible, the one you could teach next Monday with the existing outline. Ideally a multi-section course taught to a common outline, because that is where turnover lives and where a new instructor has the most scaffolding.

The adjacent course. One step from your daily work. It requires preparation, not learning. You know the content; you need to build the delivery.

The stretch course. Credible from your work history but needs genuine build time. This is the one that signals range.

The mix matters more than the individual picks. Three variations of the same course tells a chair you can fill one kind of gap. One core, one adjacent, one stretch tells them you can fill three.

Three Worked Examples

These are illustrative composites, not case files. Course titles vary by institution, so check the exact calendar entry before you use any of these labels.

The CPA controller

Fifteen years in manufacturing finance, currently a controller, no graduate degree beyond the CPA. She opens a polytechnic business diploma calendar.

Core: Introduction to Financial Accounting. First year, multiple sections every term, taught to a common outline. Typically the highest-volume, highest-turnover course in the school.

Adjacent: Managerial or Cost Accounting. Variance analysis, standard costing, budgeting. Literally her week.

Stretch: Financial Statement Analysis or Intermediate Accounting I. Defensible, but she would need to rebuild the technical detail.

Note the structure. Her core course is the one with the most sections, not the one that flatters her seniority.

The P.Eng project manager

Twelve years delivering capital projects for a utility.

Core: Project Management. Often offered in engineering technology, in business, and in continuing education. Three departments, three course codes, one qualified person.

Adjacent: Engineering Economics or Cost Estimating.

Stretch: Occupational Health and Safety or Quality Systems, where his site experience is real but his teaching-level command of the standards is thinner.

The lesson: one work history can point at three different departments' calendars, and the same claim can be filed three times.

The employment lawyer

Eight years in a labour and employment practice.

Core: Employment Law or Employment Standards inside an HR diploma or certificate. This course sits in most HR programs and is commonly taught by practitioners.

Adjacent: Business Law or Legal Environment of Business. First year, high section count.

Stretch: Labour Relations, or Human Rights and Workplace Investigations.

The counterintuitive part: the most specialized credential often maps best to the least specialized course.

Defending Each Claim From Your Work History

A chair does not care much about your job title. They care whether you can deliver the stated learning outcomes.

So work outcome by outcome. Get the course outline, read the outcomes, and map each one to a specific duty, deliverable or artifact from your work.

"I ran the monthly close" is a job description. "I prepared adjusting entries, accruals and month-end financial statements for a mid-sized manufacturing operation, which covers the adjusting-entry and statement-preparation outcomes in that course" is a defence. Same experience, completely different reading. Use your real numbers where you have them.

When your file echoes the actual outcome language from the outline, the chair reads it as someone who has done the work of understanding the course. Generic industry-experience language reads as someone who has not looked.

How to get the course outline

Many institutions post outlines publicly. If not, ask the program coordinator or the department's administrative coordinator. "I am preparing an application to teach and would like to review the current outline for ACCT-xxxx" is a normal, low-friction request.

It also puts your name in front of a person who often knows exactly which sections are short.

What to Rule Out

Filtering saves you more time than searching.

Rule out capstones and applied research courses. They carry industry partners, supervision load and institutional risk.

Rule out graduate seminars. Rule out courses with heavy licensure or accreditation constraints on who may teach them. Rule out courses that run once every two years, because there is no recurring gap. Rule out any course where the outline shows a research or theory core you cannot defend under questioning.

Then go where the demand tends to be: first and second-year service courses, evening and weekend sections, continuing education offerings, and multi-section courses taught to a common outline.

The Sanity Check Before You Commit

For each of your three courses, confirm four things.

  1. It ran in each of the last three terms. Recurring gaps beat one-off gaps.
  2. The section count. More sections, more turnover, more chance of a hole.
  3. The department. The same course title may live under three different codes in three faculties. Name the code and the department, not the topic.
  4. The posted qualification language for that course family. Not so you can self-reject, but so you know what the chair has to document.

Fifteen minutes per course. It is the highest-return work you will do in this process.

Timing: When Three Course Codes Convert Differently

The same file lands differently depending on the month. Timetable gaps become visible when a term is being built and again when someone withdraws or goes on leave. Late summer and the weeks after mid-December are the classic scramble windows.

A file with three catalogue-coded courses arriving during a scramble is a solution to a problem the chair has that day. The identical file arriving in a quiet month is a file. Send it anyway, but expect the response pattern to differ, and follow up before the next term is built rather than after.

What Per-Course Teaching Actually Pays

There is no reliable national average per-course rate in Canada, and anyone quoting one is guessing.

Here is why. Statistics Canada's University and College Academic Staff System collects data on full-time academic staff. The part-time component was last run for 1997–98 and then discontinued. There is no reliable national count of sessional and adjunct instructors, let alone their earnings.

What you can do is look up the specific agreement that governs the job. Per-course stipends are set by collective agreement and are publicly verifiable. CUPE 3902 Unit 3 at the University of Toronto and CUPE 3903 Unit 2 at York publish per-course rates. Ontario college partial-load and part-time terms are set under the OPSEU CAAT-Academic agreement.

Read the one that applies to the institution you are targeting. For broader sector context, CAUT publishes an annual Almanac of Post-Secondary Education with faculty counts, salary data and contract academic staff analysis.

One more thing worth saying plainly. For a mid-career professional, per-course teaching is a parallel track, not a reliable on-ramp to tenure track. If you pick courses using research-career logic, you will pick the wrong courses.

Who to Contact, and What If You Misjudge the Stretch

Contact the chair or the program coordinator. HR processes postings; chairs and coordinators staff sections. Do not wait for a posting, because a meaningful share of per-course assignment happens off cycle as timetables shift, after full-time load and collective agreement priority are settled.

And if you get offered the stretch course and realize you misjudged it, say so immediately. Chairs would far rather reassign a section in August than replace an instructor in October.

A candidate who flags a gap early reads as honest. A candidate who discovers it in week three reads as a staffing emergency, and that memory lasts.

The stretch claim works when it is framed as ambitious and honest. It fails when it is framed as certain.

Where to Go From Here

Open one calendar this week. Pick the institution closest to you, find the program that matches your work, and build your three: one core, one adjacent, one stretch. Then check the timetable and count the sections.

That exercise puts you ahead of most people who send a teaching inquiry, because most of them are still sending the finance email. If you want a second set of eyes on whether those three codes are actually defensible from your work history, and whether a chair can act on the file in thirty seconds, that is the conversation we have in an application audit.
