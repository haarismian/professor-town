Where to Put Your CPA, CFA, or P.Eng. on an Academic CV

By Haaris Mian September 3, 2026 9 min read

You spent years earning a designation that a hiring committee can verify in about forty seconds on a public register, and then you buried it on page four of your academic CV, between a golf club and a chamber of commerce. That is the most common self-inflicted wound I see on CVs from industry professionals applying for teaching work in Canada.

The question people ask me is a formatting question: does the CPA go after my name, in Education, or in its own section? The honest answer is that it stops being a formatting question the moment you understand what happens to your file in the first pass.

The short version. A current, regulated designation belongs in three places on an academic CV: as post-nominals after your name in the header, in a clearly named Professional Credentials and Licensure section on page one, and in the qualifications summary whenever the posting names it. Every entry needs an issuing body, a jurisdiction, and an explicit status line. Lapsed or out-of-province credentials get described, never asserted.

This post is about placement mechanics only. If your question is the prior one — whether a designation can substitute for a doctorate at all, which titles to target, and how the rest of the application should be built — read how to get a university teaching job with a CPA or P.Eng. first. What follows assumes you have already decided to apply and are now looking at your own document.

Here is the reasoning behind each of those three placements.

What the First Pass on Your Academic CV Actually Is

There is a widely quoted study from Ladders reporting that corporate recruiters spend about 7.4 seconds on a resume. That study is real, and it has nothing to do with academic hiring. I have not seen an equivalent study of academic CV screening, so treat any hard number you read about committee reading time as invented.

Here is what I can tell you from sitting on the committees.

The first pass is not reading. It is a triage exercise against a grid. Somebody, often one person rather than the full committee, builds a screening table out of the "required qualifications" section of the posting. One row per applicant. One column per posted criterion. The job on that pass is to mark each cell yes, no, or unclear, and to move.

Unclear is functionally a no. Not because anyone is hostile, but because the file with fifteen clean yeses gets read properly and yours goes into a pile that somebody may or may not return to.

So the real question is not "where is the tasteful place to put my designation." It is "where does the screener's eye land while she is filling in a cell that says CPA in good standing: Y / N."

Your Designation Is a Qualification, Not an Affiliation

The most damaging myth in this space is that a professional designation is decoration, and decoration belongs at the bottom with memberships.

On research-stream searches, maybe. On teaching-stream, sessional, and college searches in professional disciplines, your designation is frequently the screening criterion, because it is tied to something the program has to be able to demonstrate.

AACSB's 2020 business accreditation standards sort faculty into four qualification categories: Scholarly Academic, Practice Academic, Scholarly Practitioner, and Instructional Practitioner. Professional certification and licensure combined with substantial professional experience is an explicit pathway into the practitioner categories. Accredited schools report qualification status by individual and have to show coverage across the school and by discipline.

Translate that out of accreditation language. Somewhere in that business school there is a spreadsheet with a row for every instructor and a column for their qualification category. An associate dean is trying to fill in cells. Your CPA is the fastest available evidence for one of them.

Same structural logic elsewhere. Engineers Canada's CEAB accreditation criteria tie engineering design and engineering science content to the involvement and oversight of licensed professional engineers, and address the licensure of teaching staff. That is why Canadian engineering postings say "P.Eng., or eligibility for licensure in [province]." It is not a preference. It is the program protecting its accreditation.

CFA Institute's University Affiliation Program requires an affiliated degree program's curriculum to cover a substantial portion of the Candidate Body of Knowledge, and affiliated programs commonly staff those courses with charterholders and advertise that fact. CPA Canada and the provincial CPA bodies run recognition arrangements with post-secondary institutions whose courses feed CPA PEP prerequisites, and those arrangements come with expectations about who teaches.

The practical consequence: a chair who is short one practitioner-qualified instructor reads your CV completely differently than a research search chair does. She is not evaluating your intellectual trajectory. She is looking for a specific string of characters and a status.

Post-Nominals in Your Academic CV Header

Second myth: post-nominals after your name are an industry habit that academics find gauche.

What academics react badly to is a string of eight non-regulated certificate acronyms. Nobody wants to see Six Sigma belts and vendor certifications after your name. A protected professional title is a different animal, and in Canadian professional-faculty searches in accounting, finance, engineering, nursing, law, and social work, it is standard and expected in the header.

Form and order

The convention I see most often on Canadian professional-faculty CVs, and the one I would use:

Jane M. Doe, MBA, CPA, CMA

Degrees first, then regulated designations, separated by commas, no periods inside the post-nominals unless the designation itself carries them (P.Eng. does). Do not list every degree you hold. The header carries your highest relevant degree and your regulated credential. Everything else lives in Education.

One line. If your name line wraps to two lines, you have put too much on it.

The CFA-specific rule

The CFA marks are trademarks, and CFA Institute publishes a trademark usage guide governing how they may appear: proper form and capitalization, the mark following the individual's name, and no possessive use. If you hold the charter, read the current guide before you set your header. It takes ten minutes.

I will say plainly why this matters more than it seems. A finance faculty member on the committee reads a malformed CFA mark the way a peer reviewer reads a fabricated citation. It is a small thing that signals you do not know the professional norms of the field you are proposing to teach.

When the header is the wrong place

Three situations where I would keep it out of the name line:

  • Your licence is lapsed, inactive, retired, or non-practising. Describe it in a section instead. More on this below.
  • You hold the licence in a province other than the hiring institution's, and the title is protected there.
  • You are applying to a research-stream position where the credential is genuinely peripheral and your publication record is the argument.

The Standalone Credentials Section: Above or Below Education?

Name it plainly. Professional Credentials and Licensure, or Professional Designations, or Licensure. Not "Memberships and Affiliations." That heading tells the screener it is a list of things you belong to, and she will skip it.

The decision rule for placement is one question: does the posting name the credential in the required qualifications, and is the credential doing more screening work than my highest degree?

If yes, the section goes above Education. That is most sessional and college postings in accounting, finance, and engineering, and a good share of teaching-stream postings.

If no, put it immediately below Education, still on page one. For the order of everything else around it, see the scan sequence committees actually use on academic CVs.

Third myth worth killing: that listing the designation in the header and again in a section is redundant padding. It is not. They do different jobs. The header is the scan hit. The section is the verification detail: issuing body, jurisdiction, number, date, status. A screener who sees the letters on the name line and then finds the full entry two inches down has just filled in her cell with confidence and moved on. That is exactly the outcome you want.

What Goes Inside the Entry

The anatomy of a credential line has six parts. Most candidates write two of them.

  1. Designation, spelled out and abbreviated. Chartered Professional Accountant (CPA, CMA).
  2. Issuing body. CPA Ontario. APEGA. CFA Institute.
  3. Jurisdiction. The province. Non-negotiable for CPA and P.Eng.
  4. Registration or membership number. Yes, include it. It signals you expect to be verified.
  5. Date awarded.
  6. An explicit status statement. "Member in good standing." "Active, renewed annually." "Licence current."

The status line is the one nobody writes and the one that decides the cell. A designation with no status attached reads as unclear, and unclear is a no.

Status and Jurisdiction Edge Cases

Lapsed, inactive, retired, non-practising

P.Eng. and CPA are protected titles tied to current registration in a specific jurisdiction, and provincial legislation restricts use of the title to people who hold it. The CFA marks are governed by trademark rules. So a lapsed credential has to be described, never asserted.

Take a portfolio manager who left the industry in 2019 and let her CFA Institute membership lapse. She still passed all three levels and was awarded the charter. Putting "CFA" after her name is both a trademark problem and an accuracy problem.

What works:

Chartered Financial Analyst charter awarded 2011, CFA Institute. Membership currently inactive.

Honest, still signals the exam achievement, and critically, still puts the words in front of the finance group chair on page one. I have never seen a committee penalize a candidate for a clearly labelled inactive credential. I have seen committees quietly kill files over an unlabelled one.

Here is the part candidates do not think about. When a committee suspects a credential is overstated, somebody checks. The provincial CPA bodies, PEO and APEGA, and CFA Institute all maintain searchable public directories. It takes about a minute. And the damage is not confined to that one line. Once a committee finds one thing you shaded, every other unverifiable claim in the file gets re-read with suspicion.

Licensed in a different province

This one comes up constantly and almost everyone handles it badly.

An Ontario college posts a sessional design course: "P.Eng. or eligibility for licensure with PEO required." You hold a current APEGA licence in Alberta and have never registered in Ontario.

Do not write a bare "P.Eng." after your name. In Ontario that is a title-use problem, and to the engineer on the committee it reads as either sloppy or misleading.

Write the licence exactly as it exists, then address eligibility separately:

Professional Engineer (P.Eng.), APEGA, Alberta. Licence #12345. Licensed 2014, in good standing.
Eligible to apply for PEO licensure under the labour mobility provisions in Chapter 7 of the Canadian Free Trade Agreement.

The CFTA's labour mobility chapter obliges regulators to certify workers already certified for the same occupation in another province or territory without imposing significant additional requirements. That is the legal basis. Naming it does two things: it tells the chair the accreditation box can be filled, and it demonstrates you understand the regulatory environment.

In progress

"CFA Level II candidate" and "CPA PEP in progress" are legitimate entries if written precisely and placed honestly. Put them in the credentials section, clearly labelled as in progress, with the level or module and the expected completion. Do not put them in the header. Do not abbreviate them into something that could be mistaken for the completed credential. A committee that has to squint at your line to work out whether you actually hold the thing will assume you do not.

Before and After: One Credential, Two Academic CVs

Before. A controller with 22 years of experience, applying for a first sessional accounting course. Four-page CV opening with Professional Experience.

Page 4, under Memberships and Affiliations:
Chartered Professional Accountant (CPA, CMA), 2008
Rotary Club
Golf and country club

Same person, same qualification, structurally invisible to a first-pass screen. The screener gets to the end of page one, does not find the credential, marks the cell unclear, and moves on.

After.

Jane M. Doe, MBA, CPA, CMA
Toronto, ON | jane.doe@email.com

PROFESSIONAL CREDENTIALS AND LICENSURE
Chartered Professional Accountant (CPA, CMA)
CPA Ontario, member #123456. Awarded 2008.
Member in good standing, active status, renewed annually.

EDUCATION
MBA (Finance), [University], 2011
BComm, [University], 2004

Same credential. Two seconds to find instead of never.

Three Ways a Designation Still Fails the Screen

A designation gets you into the maybe pile despite a missing PhD more often than industry candidates believe. Teaching-stream, sessional, and college hiring routinely runs on the professional-qualification-plus-experience pathway, and the accreditation frameworks explicitly contemplate practitioner-qualified faculty. You do not need a doctorate to teach intro financial accounting.

But here are the three ways it fails anyway:

  1. Designation present, status ambiguous. No jurisdiction, no number, no "in good standing." The screener cannot confirm it counts.
  2. Designation present, wrong province, no eligibility note. Reads as either a misunderstanding of licensure or a misrepresentation. Both are fatal in an engineering search.
  3. Designation present, no teaching or curriculum evidence anywhere near it. You proved you are qualified in the profession and gave zero evidence you can stand in front of 90 first-years. Guest lectures, corporate training, articling-student supervision, CPD facilitation, curriculum review work. Put it on the page.

Why the Margin Got Thinner

Worth saying out loud. In January 2024 IRCC announced a national cap on study permit applications that the department expected to result in roughly 360,000 approved permits for 2024, which it described as about a 35 percent decrease from 2023. In September 2024 it announced further reductions, targeting approximately 437,000 study permits for 2025 and extending the provincial attestation letter requirement to master's and doctoral applicants.

Ontario's public colleges responded through 2024 and 2025 with program suspensions and layoffs. The sector-level counts move month to month and I am not going to quote a number I cannot stand behind, but the direction is not in dispute.

What that means for you, framed as observation and not statistic: fewer sections posted, more applicants per posting, and a first pass that has gotten noticeably harsher. When a sessional posting drew a dozen applications, a screener might have read further. At sixty applications for four sections, she will not.

The buried credential used to cost you a delay. Now it costs you the file.

One Last Thing About Your Academic CV

If you take nothing else from this: your designation is evidence against a checklist somebody is filling in, not a personal accomplishment somebody will admire. Put it where the checklist gets filled in, write it in a form a member of your own profession would recognize as correct, and attach a status to it.
