What Faculty Hiring Committees Look at in Your CV

By Haaris Mian August 25, 2026 9 min read

Table of Contents

  1. The Scan Sequence, In Order
  2. How the Same Sequence Weights Differently by Role
  3. What the Committee Does Not Discuss About Your Industry Background
  4. Role-Specific CV Checklist Before You Submit
  5. Where to Go From Here

Other posts on this site cover how to convert an industry résumé into an academic CV. This one is narrower: the scan sequence I actually use when a teaching-focused file lands in front of me, and how that sequence is weighted differently for adjunct, lecturer, and Professor of Practice roles.

If you need the document architecture itself — section order, length, language, what to drop from a corporate résumé — start with how to write an academic CV from industry and how committees read an industry candidate's CV. What follows assumes you already know those are different genres. The remaining failure mode I see is simpler: the right content is on the page, in the wrong order for the role you applied to.

I sit on faculty hiring committees at research universities. The CV is the first document I open. Not the cover letter. A cover letter attached to a CV that fails this scan may never receive serious attention, because the application has already been mentally deprioritized.

The Scan Sequence, In Order

For tenure-track research files, colleagues often start at publications and pedigree. That is not the scan I am describing. For adjunct, lecturer, and Professor of Practice roles, the teaching-focused scan I use looks like this:

1. Education. Where is it? What degrees, from what institutions? It needs to be near the top of the document. If I have to scroll past two pages of work history to find your credentials, that is already a signal — not that you lack the degree, but that you organized the file for a corporate recruiter.

I have watched this play out with an aerospace engineer who had two decades of relevant experience and a master's. He submitted a polished two-page résumé. Education was on page two, below the work history. There was no list of courses he could teach. The file moved to the secondary pile before anyone read a line of the engineering work. The content was not the problem. The scan never reached it.

2. Teaching experience. Is there a dedicated section? What courses have you taught, at what level, in what context? Guest lectures, corporate training programs, and executive education belong here if they are framed as teaching, not as "L&D initiatives." A committee member scanning for classroom evidence will not hunt for it inside a bullet about onboarding 40 analysts.

3. Courses prepared to teach. This is the section that separates industry applicants who understand academia from those who do not. A simple bulleted list — "Corporate Finance," "Supply Chain Management," "Business Ethics" — tells the committee immediately whether you map onto their curriculum. Most industry applicants do not include this section at all. That is the costly omission. I can infer from a job title that you might be able to teach a course. I cannot infer which courses, at which level, without you saying so.

Match the list to the department, not to your entire career. If the posting is for an operations course, lead with operations, logistics, and process design. Do not dump every topic you have ever touched. Specificity is the signal; a 20-item list reads as unfocused.

4. Publications, applied output, and presentations. White papers, industry reports, trade publications, conference talks, patents. Committees are looking for evidence that you generate and communicate knowledge in your field, not just execute within it. Frame these with citation logic: author, title, publication or organization, year. A "Selected Projects" block of résumé bullets does not do that job.

The cover letter, if the CV passes that scan, gets read after. Not before. In industry, a compelling narrative in a cover letter can compensate for an imperfect résumé. In academia, the CV carries most of the weight. For how to write the letter once the CV is doing that work, see how adjunct cover letters are actually read.

How the Same Sequence Weights Differently by Role

Adjunct, lecturer, and Professor of Practice are not interchangeable titles, and a single CV strategy does not work across all three. The four-item scan stays the same. What changes is which item can fail without killing the file, and which item has to be unusually strong.

Adjunct faculty

These are typically part-time, course-by-course appointments. The committee is primarily asking: can you teach this specific course competently? Publication record is the lowest-weighted of the four items. Demonstrated field expertise, applied experience, and evidence that you can manage a classroom are the primary signals. "Courses prepared to teach" is the highest-leverage section on an adjunct CV. If that list names the course in the posting, the scan often stops being a search and becomes a confirmation.

A teaching statement is useful here but rarely the deciding document. Get the course list and the teaching-experience section right first.

Lecturer roles

Lecturer appointments are usually full-time or substantial part-time roles with more curricular responsibility. The same scan now expects a fuller teaching record, not just a plausible course list. Guest lectures and corporate training still count, but the committee is looking for evidence of sustained delivery: multiple sections, a course you owned, a program you helped design.

A teaching statement carries more weight for lecturer files. Two to three paragraphs on pedagogical approach, and how your industry background informs it, should be included or appended. For the statement itself, see how to write a teaching statement as an industry professional. On the CV, the statement does not replace the teaching section. It supports it.

Professor of Practice

Research universities have created these titles to bring applied expertise into the classroom. You may be competing against people with doctorates. A PhD is not always required, particularly in professional fields like business, law, and engineering, but you need to compensate with depth of contribution: significant industry leadership, published or widely circulated applied work, evidence of shaping practice in your field.

On this scan, item 4 — publications, applied output, presentations — is no longer optional texture. It is how the committee answers "is this person an intellectual contributor, or a successful practitioner who wants a classroom?" White papers, patents, standards work, and invited talks belong here, framed as contribution, not as job history. The "no publications, no chance" assumption is a myth for these roles. Treating your applied output as if it were a résumé accomplishment list is how you revive that myth against yourself.

A CFO with a CPA and an MBA applying to teach corporate finance illustrates the difference. As an adjunct file, the scan is satisfied by Education at the top, a "Courses Prepared to Teach" list that includes Corporate Finance, and enough applied evidence that she can run a section. As a Professor of Practice file, the same career needs the deals and frameworks described as intellectual contribution — what they illustrate for students, what she has published or presented, how she has shaped practice — plus a short teaching statement. The career did not change. The weighting did.

The committee language I have heard internally when a file misses this distinction: "This is a résumé, not a CV" and "They don't seem to understand the role." Those phrases, once said in the room, are very hard to recover from.

What the Committee Does Not Discuss About Your Industry Background

This is the question most industry applicants never think to ask. In my experience, the committee does not typically have an explicit conversation about "the transition from industry to academia" as a frame for evaluating your application. The CV is expected to make that case silently through its structure.

If the document is organized like an academic CV, uses academic language, and includes the sections a committee expects to find, the committee reads it as an academic application. They evaluate your credentials on the merits. If the document looks like an industry résumé, the committee reads it as an industry application — and asks whether this person actually wants a faculty role or whether they are hedging. That mental categorization happens during the scan. It is difficult to undo with a cover letter.

Do not write a cover letter that explains why you are making the transition. Let the CV demonstrate that you already understand the environment you are entering. The letter can then focus on fit, teaching interests, and what you bring to the specific program. For the rest of the application sequence after the CV, how industry professionals actually get university teaching jobs walks the signals committees vote on once the file has survived this pass.

Role-Specific CV Checklist Before You Submit

Run the document against the scan, then against the role. Every file should clear the first list. The second list is what changes by title.

Every teaching-focused CV

  • Education section is at the top, not buried after work history
  • A dedicated Teaching Experience section exists, even if it lists guest lectures or corporate training
  • A "Courses Prepared to Teach" section is present and maps to that department's curriculum, not to your entire career
  • Industry publications, white papers, and presentations are listed in formatted citation style, not as résumé bullets
  • The document does not read as a two-page corporate résumé with "Curriculum Vitae" typed at the top

If the posting is adjunct

  • The course in the posting appears by name in "Courses Prepared to Teach"
  • Applied expertise for that course is easy to find without decoding job titles

If the posting is lecturer

  • Teaching or training evidence shows sustained delivery, not a single guest appearance
  • A brief teaching statement is included or appended

If the posting is Professor of Practice

  • Applied output is framed as intellectual contribution (publications, patents, standards, invited talks), not only as employment history
  • The file would still make sense if a colleague with a doctorate were in the same pile

If you do not have a doctorate and need the no-PhD version of this architecture, how to write a CV for a university teaching job without a PhD covers the credential substitutions. Use that piece for construction. Use this one for the order the committee will actually look.

Where to Go From Here

If you are an industry professional applying for adjunct, lecturer, or Professor of Practice roles, the next step is not another round of résumé bullets. It is a CV that puts Education, teaching, and courses prepared to teach where a committee member's eye already is — weighted for the title on the posting.
