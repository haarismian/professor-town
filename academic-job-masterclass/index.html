Module 0Start Here

Read this module in full — 15 minutes

Most people lose the academic job market before they ever submit an application. Not because they lack credentials, but because they build the wrong thing first, in the wrong order, for the wrong reader.

Here is the single most important thing in this entire document: an academic search committee is not reading your materials to find out how impressive you are. They are reading to find out whether you solve a specific problem they have. That problem is usually one of three things — a course that nobody can teach, a research area the department wants to grow, or a service gap nobody wants to fill. Every document you write is an argument that you are the answer to their problem, not a catalog of your accomplishments.

Internalize that and the rest of this masterclass becomes mechanical.

How this document is built

Eleven modules, arranged in the order you should actually do the work. You do not need to read them in sequence — the path finder below tells you where to start based on who you are. Every module contains guidance, at least one annotated real-world sample, and a checklist you can act on the same day.

Find your path

Four kinds of people apply for academic jobs. They have different assets, different liabilities, and therefore need to build their materials in a different order. Find yourself below.

Path A — The Industry Professional

You have 8+ years of real-world experience, possibly a master's or terminal professional degree, no PhD or a PhD you never used academically. You want to teach — adjunct, lecturer, professor of practice, clinical, or full-time teaching-track.
  1. Teachable zone — start here, this is your whole game
  2. Networking — you will get hired through people, not portals
  3. Academic CV — translate industry into academic form
  4. Teaching philosophy
  5. Sample syllabus — your strongest differentiator
  6. Cover letter
  7. Teaching demo
  8. Interview

Path B — The PhD Student or Postdoc

You're ABD, newly minted, or in a postdoc. You're going on the market for the first or second time. You have research credibility and thin teaching evidence.
  1. The hiring calendar — timing decides everything
  2. Networking — start 12 months before you apply
  3. Research statement — your core asset
  4. Academic CV
  5. Cover letter — customized per posting
  6. Teaching philosophy + syllabus
  7. Research talk — practice 10+ times
  8. Full interview prep

Path C — The Adjunct or Contingent Faculty

You're already teaching, but per-course, at one or several institutions. You want a full-time line: lecturer, teaching professor, or tenure-track.
  1. Academic branding — you must become known for something specific
  2. Networking — internal advocacy is your fastest lever
  3. Academic CV — restructure to foreground teaching scale
  4. Teaching philosophy — evidence-driven, not aspirational
  5. Course evaluations — how to present them
  6. Cover letter
  7. Teaching demo + interview

Path D — The Current Professor (Lateral Move)

You hold a faculty line and want a different one — better institution, better fit, better geography, or an administrative step up.
  1. Networking — discreet, senior, and slow. Read this first.
  2. Thought leadership — visibility is how lateral offers find you
  3. Research statement — reframed as trajectory, not promise
  4. Academic CV — pruned, not padded
  5. Cover letter — must answer "why leave?"
  6. Interview + negotiation
If you're short on time

Everyone, regardless of path, should do these three things this week: (1) write your teachable zone statement, (2) send three outreach messages, (3) get your CV into the standard academic format. Those three moves generate more momentum than a month of polishing.

The hiring calendar

Academic hiring runs on a calendar that has almost nothing to do with when you feel ready. Missing the window costs you a full year. The rhythm below is typical for North American institutions; adjust by roughly one term for UK/EU/AUS cycles, which are more rolling.

When What's happening What you should be doing
Jun–Aug Departments finalize which lines they'll get to run Networking. Informational calls. Build your master documents. This is the highest-leverage and least-used window.
Aug–Oct Peak posting season for tenure-track Apply. Tailor aggressively. Confirm your referees.
Oct–Dec Long lists, first-round Zoom screens Screen prep. 5-minute research pitch rehearsed cold.
Jan–Mar Campus visits, job talks, teaching demos Full interview prep. Travel. Thank-you notes.
Feb–Apr Offers and negotiation Negotiate. Know your walk-away number.
Apr–Aug Failed searches, late resignations, sudden needs The "second wave." Real jobs, less competition, faster decisions. Do not check out in April.
Rolling Adjunct, lecturer, and professor-of-practice needs These are filled 2–8 weeks before term starts, often by whoever the chair already knows. See Module 8.
The most common timing mistake

Waiting until your dissertation is defended, your paper is accepted, or your materials are "perfect" before you start networking. The people who get hired started conversations six to twelve months before the posting existed. Networking is the only part of this process with a lead time you cannot compress.

What "fit" actually means

You will hear the word fit constantly, usually as the explanation for a rejection. It sounds like a polite non-answer. It isn't. Fit has four measurable components, and you can influence three of them:

  • Curricular fit. Can you cover the courses on their teaching schedule, including the unglamorous required ones? This is the most decisive and the most ignorable-in-your-favor. A candidate who can teach the required stats course beats a more distinguished candidate who can't.
  • Intellectual fit. Does your work complement rather than duplicate existing faculty? Committees avoid hiring a second version of someone they have.
  • Institutional fit. Do you understand what kind of place this is? A research-intensive R1 and a regional teaching-focused comprehensive want opposite things in the same document. Applying to both with the same letter is the single most common self-inflicted wound.
  • Collegial fit. Will people want to sit in a curriculum committee meeting with you for twenty years? Unfair, unfalsifiable, and completely real.

Everything in Module 1 onward is the practice of demonstrating those four things on paper and in person.

Module 1Academic Branding

Branding in academia is not self-promotion. It is the discipline of being legible — of making it effortless for a stranger to say, in one sentence, what you do and why they'd want you in their department.

Here is the test. A search committee member is walking to the parking lot with a colleague and says, "We should look at that candidate — the one who—". Whatever comes after that dash is your brand. If nobody can finish that sentence, you don't have one. And if you don't supply the ending, they will invent one, and it will be something forgettable like "the one from Michigan."

Most candidates fail this test because they describe themselves by category ("I'm an organizational behavior scholar," "I'm a data scientist") rather than by the specific problem they own. Category descriptions put you in a bucket with two hundred other applicants. Problem ownership puts you in a bucket of one.

Identifying your exact teachable zone and expertise

Your teachable zone is the intersection of three things: what you have genuine authority in, what students need to learn, and what a department actually has to staff. It is not the same as your research area, and for anyone on Path A or Path C it matters far more than your research area.

The three-circle test
  • Circle 1 — Earned authority. What can you speak about for two hours with no notes, and defend under challenge from someone who does it professionally? Authority comes from doing, from studying, or from both — but it must be demonstrable.
  • Circle 2 — Curricular demand. What courses exist in catalogs, appear in job postings, or are being added to programs right now? This is a research question, not a guess. You go look.
  • Circle 3 — Staffing pain. What do departments struggle to cover? Usually: quantitative methods, required intro courses at scale, anything requiring current industry tooling, capstone/practicum supervision, and online or asynchronous delivery.

Your teachable zone is where all three overlap. If you only have Circles 1 and 2, you're competing on prestige. If you have all three, you're competing on scarcity — and scarcity wins.

Step 1 — Inventory your authority (90 minutes)

Open a blank document. Do not edit while you do this. List:

  • Every project, engagement, study, or product you've owned end to end. For each, write the decision you were trusted to make.
  • Every tool, method, framework, or technique you can operate at an expert level — not "familiar with," but "have used to produce a real outcome."
  • Every topic on which someone has asked you for advice more than three times. This list is more valuable than the other two, because it's external evidence of authority.
  • Every mistake you've made professionally that taught you something a textbook wouldn't. These become your best teaching material.
  • If you have research: the three questions your work has actually answered, in plain language, no jargon.

Step 2 — Map real curricular demand (2 hours, and it must be real research)

This is the step nearly everyone skips, and it is the step that produces the advantage.

  1. Pull 15–25 job postings in your target space from HigherEdJobs, the Chronicle, Interfolio, discipline-specific job wikis, and — most importantly — the HR pages of the 20 specific institutions you'd actually take a job at.
  2. Extract every named course from the "teaching needs" sections. Put them in a spreadsheet. Count frequency.
  3. Then go to the course catalogs of those same 20 institutions. Find the courses in your area. Note which ones are required vs. elective, and which ones are listed but not offered this year — a listed-but-unoffered course is a staffing gap with a course number already attached to it.
  4. Look at the faculty roster. Who currently teaches what? Who is emeritus or about to be? Which areas have exactly one person covering them? A department with one methods person is a department that is one retirement away from a crisis.
What you're looking for

A course that appears in five or more postings, sits in a required sequence, and is currently covered by one person or nobody. That course is your wedge. When you can say "I can teach your required Business Analytics course, and here's the syllabus," you have moved from applicant to solution.

Step 3 — Write the zone statement

Compress the overlap into a single sentence in this shape:

I teach [SPECIFIC COURSE AREA] to [SPECIFIC STUDENT POPULATION], drawing on [SPECIFIC EARNED AUTHORITY], with a focus on [THE DISTINCTIVE ANGLE ONLY YOU BRING].
Weak — category description

"I'm a marketing professional with 15 years of experience interested in teaching marketing courses at the university level."

Why it fails: Describes a category, not a problem. Interchangeable with 400 other people. Gives the committee nothing to remember or act on.

Strong — problem ownership

"I teach marketing analytics and consumer research methods to undergraduate business students, drawing on twelve years running measurement at consumer brands, with a focus on the gap between what analytics tools report and what actually drives decisions."

Why it works: Names two teachable courses, a student population, verifiable authority, and a point of view. A chair reads this and immediately knows which line on the schedule you fill.

Step 4 — Build your course coverage list

Every candidate should have a standing list of courses they can teach, organized in three tiers. This list goes into cover letters, informational calls, and interviews, and it is the thing chairs remember.

Tier Definition How many
Tier 1 — Ready now You could teach it next term with two weeks' notice. You have or could produce a full syllabus immediately. 3–5 courses
Tier 2 — Ready with prep You have the substantive knowledge; you'd need a term to build the course. Includes required/service courses you may not love but can absolutely deliver. 4–6 courses
Tier 3 — New courses I'd propose Courses that don't exist in their catalog that you could create, tied to a demonstrable student or program need. Signals initiative and curricular thinking. 2–3 courses
Do not skip Tier 2

Candidates love to talk about the exciting new seminar they'd design. Chairs are far more moved by "I can take the 120-seat required Intro course off your hands." Willingness to teach the unglamorous course is the fastest way to become the practical choice. Say it explicitly and without apology.

Step 5 — Name your through-line

A through-line is the idea that connects everything you do — teaching, research, and practice. It's what makes a portfolio feel coherent rather than assembled. It usually takes the form of a claim you'd defend:

  • "Quantitative training fails students when it's divorced from the decision it informs."
  • "Design education overweights the artifact and underweights the constraint."
  • "Public health interventions fail at the implementation layer, not the evidence layer."

You'll use the through-line in your teaching philosophy opening, your research statement framing, your cover letter close, and the answer to "tell us about yourself." One idea, four documents. That repetition is what makes someone memorable.

Thought leadership

Thought leadership in academia is not posting takes. It is producing artifacts that make you findable and citable by the specific people who make hiring decisions. The goal is that when a chair Googles you before the screen call, they find evidence that you're a serious person with a point of view. That's it. That's the whole objective.

The visibility ladder

Work up this ladder. Each rung is more effort and more credibility than the last. You do not need the top rungs to get hired; you need the bottom three to not be invisible.

Rung Artifact Effort Why it matters
1 A clean personal site: bio, CV PDF, courses taught, teaching statement, contact 1 weekend This is the baseline. Its absence is a negative signal in 2026.
2 Google Scholar + ORCID (if you publish); LinkedIn kept current (if you don't) 2 hours Makes your record verifiable in ten seconds.
3 Consistent short public writing in your zone — a newsletter, a blog, LinkedIn essays 1–2 hrs/wk Demonstrates you can explain, which is the core teaching skill.
4 Conference presentations, panels, invited workshops Per event Puts you in rooms with the people who staff searches. See Module 8.
5 Practitioner publications, trade press, op-eds, podcast appearances Variable Especially powerful for Paths A and C — it's the industry equivalent of a citation record.
6 Peer-reviewed publications, book chapters, edited volumes Months–years Non-negotiable for R1 tenure-track; helpful but not required elsewhere.
7 A book, a widely-adopted tool, a named framework Years Turns you into a person departments recruit rather than screen.

The one-topic rule

Pick one topic and stay on it for at least twelve months. The instinct is to demonstrate range. Range makes you forgettable. If everything you publish for a year sits inside your teachable zone, then by month twelve, people in your field associate that topic with your name — and association is the entire mechanism by which opportunities arrive unsolicited.

A realistic cadence that works

One substantive piece per month (800–1,500 words) in your zone, published anywhere with a stable URL. Twelve pieces a year gives you: a body of work to point to, twelve reasons to reach out to people (see Module 8), material for your teaching philosophy, and a demonstration of the exact skill — explaining hard things clearly — that teaching evaluates.

Turning content into course material

The highest-leverage move: write your public pieces as teaching modules in disguise. Each piece should be a concept, an example, a common misunderstanding, and a takeaway. Do this twelve times and you have simultaneously built visibility, a course, and the evidence base for your teaching philosophy. Nothing is wasted.

What to avoid

  • Credential theater. Listing affiliations and titles without producing anything. Committees discount it instantly.
  • Fighting on the internet. Search committees Google you. Public conflict, however justified, reads as collegiality risk.
  • Range-signaling. Publishing across five unrelated topics to seem broad. Reads as unfocused.
  • Waiting for the perfect piece. Twelve adequate pieces beat one excellent piece you never finished.

Module 2The Academic CV

A résumé argues. A CV documents. Confusing the two is the most common and most immediately visible error a candidate coming from industry makes — and it is also, quietly, a mistake plenty of PhDs make in the other direction.

Résumé Academic CV
Length 1–2 pages, strictly As long as your record — 2 pages early career, 40+ pages senior
Purpose Persuade with selected highlights Document the complete scholarly and professional record
Voice Achievement bullets, action verbs Neutral, factual, reverse-chronological entries
Tailoring Rewritten per application Largely stable; lightly reordered per application
Design Can be designed Plain. Design is a negative signal.
Omissions Expected Read as gaps or concealment

What makes a good CV

A good academic CV does four things. In order of how often candidates get them wrong:

1. It is scannable in 45 seconds

Committee members review 80–250 applications. Your CV gets a first pass measured in seconds, and that pass is looking for four things: degree and field, publication/teaching record, institutional history, and whether your record matches the level of the position. If those four aren't findable without scrolling and squinting, you lose on formatting alone.

Practically: consistent heading hierarchy, generous white space, dates in a fixed position on every line, no dense paragraphs, no two-column layouts that break when printed, no graphics.

2. It is ordered by what this specific job weighs most

The sections after your education can be reordered, and should be. This is the only meaningful tailoring an academic CV needs.

  • Research-intensive posting: Education → Appointments → Publications → Grants → Presentations → Teaching → Service
  • Teaching-focused posting: Education → Teaching Experience → Courses Taught → Teaching Awards/Evaluations → Curriculum Development → Publications → Service
  • Practitioner/professional school posting: Education → Professional Experience → Teaching Experience → Courses Taught → Professional Credentials → Publications & Practitioner Writing → Service

3. It quantifies where quantification is meaningful

Academic CVs are factual, not boastful — but facts include numbers. Numbers convert vague experience into verifiable scale.

Vague

Instructor, Introduction to Statistics
Department of Sociology, 2021–2024

Specific

Instructor of Record, SOC 210: Introduction to Statistics
Department of Sociology, 2021–2024
6 sections · 40–75 students per section · 310 students total · mean evaluation 4.6/5.0 (dept. mean 4.1) · redesigned course to project-based assessment in 2022

4. It is scrupulously honest and internally consistent

Every date, title, and claim will be checked against your LinkedIn, your website, your letters, and your references. Inconsistency — even innocent inconsistency — is the fastest way to be quietly removed from a list.

Category integrity is non-negotiable

Never list a manuscript under "Publications" if it is under review. Never list a talk you didn't give. Never list a grant you were named on but did not receive. Use precise status labels: published, in press, accepted, revise and resubmit, under review, in preparation. Committees contain people who will recognize a padded entry immediately, and a single one contaminates the entire document.

Below is the full menu. Not every section applies to every person — include a section only if you have real content for it. An empty or near-empty section draws attention to the gap.

The section most people should add and don't

"Courses Prepared to Teach." Six to ten course titles, using the naming conventions of the institutions you're applying to. It takes twenty minutes and it directly answers the question the chair is actually asking. If you do one thing from this module, do this.

Special guidance by path

Path A — translating industry into academic form

Your problem is not lack of substance; it's that your substance is in the wrong vocabulary. Translate:

Industry experience Academic CV framing
Trained new hires, ran onboarding Curriculum development and instruction — list under Teaching Experience with participant counts and hours
Client workshops, conference talks Invited Talks / Professional Presentations, with full citation formatting
Internal white papers, industry reports Technical Reports and Practitioner Publications — a legitimate, named CV section
Managed teams and budgets Professional Experience with scope line; also signals administrative capacity for program-director roles
Board or association service Service — Professional/Disciplinary
Mentored junior staff Mentorship, with number of mentees and duration

Two more rules for Path A: drop the résumé bullets (achievement-verb bullets look out of place; use scope lines instead), and do not shorten to two pages — a two-page academic CV from a twenty-year professional reads as a thin record, not a disciplined one.

Path B — the early-career CV

Yours will be 2–5 pages and that is correct. Do not pad. Instead: include "in preparation" work with honest labels, list conference presentations fully, list every course including TA work with the role labeled precisely, and include departmental service (grad student rep, seminar organizer) — it shows citizenship.

Path C — foregrounding teaching scale

Your CV's job is to convert "adjunct" from a status into a body of evidence. Aggregate: total students taught, number of distinct course preparations, number of institutions, evaluation means against departmental means, courses you redesigned. "Taught 14 distinct courses across 3 institutions to 2,100 students since 2018" is a serious record and most adjunct CVs never state it.

Path D — pruning, not padding

Senior CVs get long and lose their shape. Reverse-chronological within sections, cut nothing factual, but move dated material (graduate teaching, decade-old service) below current material and consider a brief "Selected" designation for talks if the list exceeds a page. Add sections that signal readiness for the level you're seeking — external funding, doctoral supervision, program leadership, editorial roles.

Why this sample works
  • Areas of Expertise sits at the top — the reader knows the teachable zone in five seconds.
  • Teaching precedes Professional Experience because the target is a teaching-focused school. For a professional-school practitioner line, those two would swap.
  • Courses Prepared to Teach uses the target school's course naming and includes both required workhorses and a proposed elective.
  • Evaluations are stated against the departmental mean — a raw 4.7 is meaningless without context.
  • Industry roles use scope lines, not bullets — team size, portfolio value, what was built.
  • The redesign story appears as a fact, not a brag: enrollment number, adoption by other courses. Evidence, not adjectives.
  • Practitioner writing is its own honestly-labeled section — not smuggled in under "Publications."

Formatting rules

  • File format: PDF, always. Name it Lastname_Firstname_CV.pdf. Never send a .docx unless explicitly requested.
  • Font: One serif (Garamond, Palatino, Times) or one clean sans (Calibri, Helvetica). 11–12pt body.
  • Margins: 1 inch. Do not shrink margins to fit — length is not a sin here.
  • Page numbers and running header with your name on every page after the first. Pages get separated.
  • Dates right-aligned or in a consistent left column — pick one and never deviate.
  • No tables with visible borders, no columns, no color, no logos, no headshot, no skill bars.
  • Hyperlinks on your website, DOIs, and ORCID — live and tested.
  • Update it every single time something happens. A CV maintained continuously takes ten minutes a month; a CV reconstructed under deadline takes two days and has errors.

Module 3The Cover Letter

The CV documents. The cover letter argues. It is the only document in your packet where you get to tell the committee what the evidence means — and it is, empirically, the document that decides whether anyone reads the rest.

Committee members typically read the cover letter first, and it functions as a filter. A generic letter gets your file moved to the "no" pile before your CV is opened. A specific letter earns your CV a careful read. That asymmetry is why 45 minutes of tailoring per application beats 45 more applications sent generically.

The single most common failure

A letter that could be sent to any institution. If you can swap the university name and the letter still works, it is not a cover letter — it's a personal statement, and committees have learned to discard those. Every letter must contain at least three facts that are only true of this institution.

The five-paragraph architecture

Length: 1.5 to 2 pages for teaching-focused roles, 2 pages for research-intensive. One page reads as insufficient effort. Three pages reads as an inability to prioritize.

¶ Job Length
1 The hook and the claim. Name the position and where you saw it. State in one sentence what you'd bring. Name one specific thing about them. 4–6 lines
2 Your primary asset. Research for R1; teaching for teaching-focused; practitioner expertise for professional schools. Lead with whatever the posting weighs most. 1/3 page
3 Your secondary asset. The other half of the pair. Never omit it — a research letter with no teaching paragraph signals you'll be a problem to schedule. 1/4 page
4 The fit paragraph. The most important paragraph in the letter and the one most people write worst. Specific named connections to their department, courses, centers, students, and colleagues. 1/3 page
5 Service, close, and logistics. Brief service/citizenship note, thank you, availability, materials enclosed. 4–5 lines

Paragraph 1 — the hook

Do not open with "I am writing to apply for..." — it wastes the highest-attention sentence in the document. But do name the position and requisition number somewhere in the first two sentences, because HR systems and committee chairs both need it.

Dead opening

"I am writing to express my strong interest in the Assistant Professor of Marketing position at your university, which I saw advertised on HigherEdJobs. I believe my background makes me an excellent candidate for this role."

Live opening

"I am applying for the Assistant Teaching Professor of Marketing position (Req. 2026-0413) because your program does something unusual: it requires every marketing major to complete an analytics sequence before the capstone. I have spent twelve years building measurement functions for consumer brands, and the gap that sequence is designed to close — between what analytics reports and what decisions actually require — is the specific thing I teach."

Paragraph 4 — the fit paragraph

This paragraph is where letters are won and lost. It must contain named specifics. Generic praise ("your department's excellent reputation") is worse than nothing because it signals you didn't look.

Sources for genuine specifics, in descending order of value:

  1. The course catalog. Name two or three specific courses by number and title that you're prepared to teach. Nothing else in the letter is this persuasive.
  2. The posting itself. If it names courses, centers, or program needs, address each one explicitly.
  3. Faculty pages. Name one or two people and a real point of complementarity — not flattery. "Dr. Okafor's work on X connects to my Y in the following way." Never name someone whose work you haven't read.
  4. The strategic plan / dean's letter / accreditation report. Almost always public, almost never read by applicants. It tells you exactly what the institution says it's trying to become.
  5. Student profile and mission. If it's an HSI, an MSI, a heavily first-generation or transfer-heavy student body, a religious institution, or a workforce-aligned college — say so, and say what you'd do about it concretely.
  6. Anything you learned in a conversation. If you did Module 8 properly, you have information the other applicants don't.
The reverse-engineering method

Paste the job posting into a two-column table. Left column: every requirement, phrase, and named need, verbatim. Right column: your specific evidence for each. Any left-column cell with an empty right column is either a gap to address or a reason not to apply. Then write the letter from the right column. This takes 20 minutes and it is the difference between a tailored letter and a letter that merely mentions the university's name.

Path-specific letter moves

Path The question your letter must answer How to answer it
A · Industry professional "Can this person actually teach, or do they just know things?" Give teaching evidence early and concretely — guest lectures, training, adjunct sections, workshop design. Then convert industry experience into pedagogical assets: real datasets, live cases, employer network, placement.
B · PhD / postdoc "Is there a real five-year trajectory here, or just a dissertation?" Describe the dissertation in two sentences, then spend more words on what comes after it — the next two projects, the funding path, the second-book or second-paper arc.
C · Adjunct / contingent "Why hasn't this person been hired full-time already?" Never apologize or explain the market. Instead, present scale as an asset: breadth of preparations, evaluation record, curriculum work, and the specific reason a full-time line lets you do something you currently can't.
D · Current professor "Why are you leaving, and will you actually come?" Answer "why leave" in one forward-looking sentence — never negative about your current institution. Then make the affirmative case: what this place has that enables the next stage of your work. Address geography honestly if relevant.

Module 4The Teaching Philosophy

Almost every teaching philosophy statement is bad, and they are bad in exactly the same way: they describe values instead of practices. "I believe in creating an inclusive, student-centered learning environment where all students can reach their potential" is a sentence that appears in thousands of statements and distinguishes none of them.

A teaching philosophy earns attention when it makes a falsifiable claim about how learning works in your subject, and then shows what you concretely do because you believe that. Claim → practice → evidence. Everything else is filler.

The specifications
  • Length: 1–2 pages. One page is fine and often better. Two pages maximum, always.
  • Voice: First person, present tense, plain language.
  • Structure: Prose, not bullets. Headers are acceptable but not required.
  • Content ratio: Roughly 25% claim, 50% concrete practice, 25% evidence and future intent.
  • Tailoring: Light. Adjust the student population you describe and the courses you name; keep the core stable.

The five-move structure

Move 1 — Open with a claim, not a value (1 paragraph)

Your opening should state something about learning in your discipline that a reasonable colleague could disagree with. Disagreement is what makes it a position rather than a platitude.

A value (unfalsifiable)

"I believe every student can succeed with the right support. My classroom is a place where curiosity is nurtured and students feel safe to take intellectual risks."

Nobody disagrees with this, which means it says nothing.

A claim (arguable)

"Students do not fear statistics because it is difficult. They fear it because they have only ever been asked to compute, never to decide. My teaching is built on the conviction that quantitative anxiety dissolves when the method arrives second and the decision arrives first."

A colleague could argue the opposite. That's why it's worth reading.

Finding your claim

Answer this: "What do most people get wrong about teaching my subject?" Your honest answer to that question, written as a declarative sentence, is your opening claim. If you're on Path A, an even better prompt: "What did school teach me about this subject that turned out to be wrong once I was actually doing the work?"

Move 2 — Show the practice that follows from the claim (2–3 paragraphs)

This is the bulk of the statement and where most people go abstract. Be granular enough that a reader could picture your classroom. Name the assignment. Name the sequence. Name what students actually do on a Tuesday.

Good specificity looks like: "In week three, before students have seen a formula, I give them a dataset and a decision — a marketing director must choose between two package designs — and ask them to defend a recommendation in writing. They argue. They discover they need a way to distinguish signal from noise. Only then do we build the t-test, and it arrives as an answer to a question they already have."

Cover, across your paragraphs:

  • How you sequence material — and why in that order
  • What students actually produce — the assignments, projects, artifacts
  • How you assess — and what your assessment choices reveal about what you value
  • How you handle the students who struggle — specific mechanisms, not sentiment
  • What you do differently for different populations — first-generation, transfer, working, returning, ESL, neurodivergent students

Move 3 — Give evidence that it works (1 paragraph)

Claims plus practices plus no evidence reads as theory. Evidence can be:

  • Evaluation data, with departmental comparison
  • Direct quotes from student evaluations (two short ones, maximum — more reads as insecure)
  • Outcome data: pass rates, DFW rate changes, enrollment changes, downstream course performance, placement
  • Adoption: other instructors using your materials, department adopting your structure
  • Peer observation notes or teaching awards

Move 4 — Show that you revise (1 short paragraph)

The most underrated move in a teaching philosophy: describe something that didn't work and what you changed. It signals reflective practice, which is precisely what the genre is supposed to demonstrate and almost nobody actually does. Two or three sentences is enough.

Move 5 — Point forward (1 short paragraph)

What you want to teach next, what you want to learn about teaching, and — briefly — what you'd contribute to this specific program. This is the only lightly-tailored part.

Annotated sample teaching philosophy

Sample — one page, Path A candidate, quantitative business course

Teaching Philosophy — Jordan A. Reyes

Students do not fear analytics because it is difficult. They fear it because they have been asked, repeatedly, to compute without ever being asked to decide. Twelve years of hiring analysts has convinced me that the failure mode of quantitative education is not insufficient rigor — it is a sequencing error. We teach the method first and the decision last, and by the time the decision arrives most students have concluded the subject is not for them. My teaching inverts that order.

In MKTG 3320, students encounter a decision before they encounter a technique. In the third week, before anyone has seen a formula, I hand out two package designs, six weeks of sales data from a real launch, and a memo from a fictional VP who needs an answer by Friday. Students make a recommendation and defend it in writing. Then they read each other's memos, and they find that three groups reached opposite conclusions from identical data. That argument is the actual lesson. The t-test we build the following week does not arrive as a hurdle; it arrives as the thing they already needed. I have used this structure for every quantitative concept in the course — sampling, confidence, regression, experimental design — and the pattern holds: motivation before mechanics.

The course culminates in a semester-long client engagement. Student teams receive a genuine brief from a local firm, work with real and therefore messy data, and present a recommendation to the actual decision-maker in week fourteen. I grade the defensibility of the recommendation, not the sophistication of the method — a well-reasoned cross-tabulation that answers the question outscores an elegant model that answers a different one. This choice is deliberate and I tell students why: in practice, the analyst who can explain a simple answer is more valuable than the one who can produce a complicated one.

Students arrive at this course with sharply different quantitative preparation, and I have found that the gap is more often confidence than capability. I front-load low-stakes, high-frequency practice: weekly five-minute problems that are graded for completion rather than accuracy, so that being wrong in week two costs nothing. I hold what I call "no-question-too-basic" hours, and I open them by asking a basic question myself. Since instituting these, the DFW rate in my sections has fallen from 14% to 6%, and my evaluation mean is 4.7 against a school mean of 4.2. One student wrote: "I came in having failed statistics twice. I'm now applying for analyst jobs." The department adopted the client-project structure for two additional courses in 2024.

Not everything has worked. My first version of the client project let teams choose their own clients, which produced wildly uneven experiences — one team spent six weeks waiting on a contact who never replied. I now source and vet all briefs myself before the term begins, which is more work for me and a far more equitable experience for students. I made a similar correction on peer evaluation, which I initially weighted too heavily and which advantaged confident students over careful ones.

I want to keep pushing on the earliest part of the sequence — the transition from intuition to formalism — and I am currently reading and writing on how that transition works for students without prior quantitative confidence. In a program that requires an analytics sequence before the capstone, I would want to make sure the two connect explicitly, so that students arrive at the capstone already knowing what a defensible recommendation looks like.

Annotation
  • ¶1 makes an arguable claim and grounds it in a source of authority (hiring analysts) that only this candidate has.
  • ¶2 is granular enough to picture. A specific week, a specific artifact, a specific student experience, and the pedagogical principle named at the end.
  • ¶3 states an assessment philosophy with a trade-off — grading defensibility over sophistication — which reveals values through a choice rather than an adjective.
  • ¶4 addresses the struggling student with mechanisms (low-stakes frequency, framing of office hours) and then supplies real evidence: DFW rate change, evaluation with comparison, one short quote, and adoption by colleagues.
  • ¶5 is the paragraph almost nobody writes. Two concrete failures and two concrete corrections. This is the strongest signal of reflective practice in the document.
  • ¶6 points forward and connects to the specific program's curricular structure in one sentence. Light tailoring, high effect.
  • Notice what's absent: "student-centered," "lifelong learning," "safe space," "passion," "inclusive environment." The statement demonstrates all of those things without claiming any of them.

Path-specific notes

  • Path A: Your practitioner authority is your differentiator, but it can read as "war stories." Anchor every industry reference to a pedagogical purpose. The claim in ¶1 should come from something you learned by doing the work.
  • Path B: Thin teaching experience is fine — but be honest about scope and specific about what you did. TA-ing a discussion section well, described precisely, beats an inflated claim. Draw on what you learned as a student in your field: what confused you, and what you'd do about it.
  • Path C: You have the most evidence of anyone and usually undersell it. Aggregate across institutions. Note where you've taught the same course to different populations — that comparison is genuinely interesting and almost nobody has it.
  • Path D: Include mentorship and doctoral supervision, and include evidence of teaching development at the program level: curriculum you built, colleagues you've mentored, assessment work.
Phrases to cut on sight

"Student-centered." "Lifelong learners." "Meet students where they are." "Guide on the side, not sage on the stage." "Passionate about teaching." "Foster a love of learning." "Create a safe space." "Diverse learning styles" (the learning-styles model lacks empirical support, and specialists on committees know it). None of these are wrong as sentiments; all of them are invisible as writing.

  • At least two specific classroom practices described at week-level granularity
  • Assessment approach explained with the trade-off made explicit
  • Concrete mechanisms for supporting struggling and underprepared students
  • Evidence paragraph with numbers and comparison context
  • A paragraph describing something that failed and what you changed
  • Forward-looking paragraph with one specific connection to this program
  • Every banned phrase removed
  • Under 2 pages, PDF

Module 5The Research Statement

The research statement answers one question: if we hire you, what will exist in five years that doesn't exist now? It is a document about the future that uses the past as evidence. Most candidates write the reverse — a memoir of completed work with a vague gesture at "future directions" in the last paragraph — and it fails.

The specifications
  • Length: 2–3 pages for research-intensive; 1–2 pages for teaching-focused institutions; 1 page or a merged section for practitioner roles.
  • Structure: Headers are helpful and standard here (unlike the teaching philosophy).
  • Audience: Written for an intelligent non-specialist in your broad field — a committee usually contains at most one person in your subfield.
  • Ratio: Roughly 40% past work, 50% forward agenda, 10% infrastructure and fit.

Structure

1. The through-line (1 paragraph)

Open with the question that organizes everything you've done and will do — the intellectual problem, not the topic. "I study how organizations make decisions when the data they have does not answer the question they face" is a through-line. "I study organizational decision-making" is a topic. The through-line makes disparate projects cohere; the topic makes them look scattered.

The jargon test

Read your first paragraph to someone with a bachelor's degree in an unrelated field. If they can restate your research question back to you, it passes. If they can't, rewrite it. Committee members outside your subfield are the ones who decide whether your file advances, and they cannot advocate for work they can't summarize.

2. Completed work — organized as a line, not a list (2–4 paragraphs)

Group your work into two or three streams, and show how each answered part of the through-line question. For each stream: the question, the approach, the finding, and — critically — what it opened up. That last element is what turns a retrospective into a trajectory.

Cite your own work inline so the reader can map claims to CV lines. Name the venue when it's strong.

3. The forward agenda — the actual point of the document (3–5 paragraphs)

This is where you win or lose. Describe two or three specific projects with enough operational detail that a committee believes they will happen. For each:

  • The question and why it matters now
  • The approach — method, data, site, population
  • Current status — data collected, pilot run, IRB submitted, draft in progress. Concreteness here is everything.
  • Expected outputs — target venues, named honestly
  • Timeline — a realistic year-by-year arc
  • Funding — specific programs you'd target, and whether you've submitted before
Vague future

"In the future, I plan to extend my dissertation work by examining these dynamics in additional organizational contexts and pursuing external funding to support this research."

Credible future

"My next project extends this to municipal governments, where the decision environment differs in a specific and testable way: officials face public accountability for the decision process itself, not only the outcome. I have secured agreements with three mid-sized city administrations, submitted IRB in January, and will begin interviews in fall 2026. I expect two papers — one methodological, targeted to Public Administration Review, and one empirical, targeted to JPART — with the first submitted by summer 2027. I will submit an NSF SES proposal in the August 2026 cycle; my 2024 submission scored in the fundable range but was not funded, and I have the panel feedback to address."

4. Infrastructure and fit (1 paragraph)

What you need to do this work — lab space, computing, archival access, course release, travel — stated modestly and realistically. Then what their institution offers that enables it: a center, a data resource, a colleague, a student population, a partnership. This paragraph also quietly signals that you understand what you cost.

5. Students and research (1 short paragraph)

Frequently omitted and always noticed. How will students participate? Undergraduate research assistants at a liberal arts college; doctoral supervision at an R1; capstone teams at a professional school. Name specific tasks students could do and what they'd get out of it.

Module 6Making a Sample Syllabus

The sample syllabus is the most underused document in the academic job search. Most postings don't require one. Most candidates therefore don't send one. Which is exactly why sending one works.

A syllabus is the only document in your packet that is operational. Everything else describes what you would do. A syllabus is what you would do — a fourteen-week commitment, written down, gradeable, and immediately usable. When a chair is choosing between two candidates and one of them has already handed over a working version of the course they need staffed, that is not a small advantage.

When to send one unasked

Send it when the posting names a specific course you're targeting, when you're applying to a teaching-focused institution, when you're Path A or Path C, or when you're proposing a new course you want them to want. Attach it as a clearly labeled supplementary document and mention it in the cover letter's final paragraph. If the application portal has a single "additional materials" slot, combine it with your teaching portfolio into one PDF.

Choosing which course to build

Choice When it's right Risk
The required course they struggle to staff Almost always the best choice. Highest practical value to the chair. Low. You may be compared to an existing version — so read their catalog description carefully.
An existing course in their catalog When you want to show you'd slot in immediately Must match their catalog description, credit hours, and level. Deviating without acknowledgment reads as carelessness.
A new course you'd propose When paired with one of the above, or when your teachable zone is genuinely distinctive Alone, it can read as "wants to teach only what interests them." Never send only this.

The strongest packet contains two: one required workhorse course and one proposed elective. That combination says "I will carry the load, and I will also build something."

Anatomy of a strong sample syllabus

1. Header block

Course number and title matching their conventions, credit hours, term, meeting pattern, modality, prerequisites, instructor name, contact, office hours. Getting the course number format right signals you looked at their catalog.

2. Course description (1 paragraph)

If the course exists in their catalog, start from their official description and extend it. Do not contradict it.

3. Learning outcomes (4–6, measurable)

This is where syllabi are judged by anyone with curriculum or accreditation experience — and every department has one of those people on the committee. Outcomes must use observable verbs and must map to assessments.

Not measurable

"Students will understand the fundamentals of marketing analytics and appreciate the role of data in business decisions."

You cannot observe "understand" or "appreciate."

Measurable

"By the end of this course, students will be able to: (1) select an appropriate analytical method given a stated business decision and defend the choice in writing; (2) execute descriptive and inferential analyses on messy real-world data using R; (3) communicate an analytical recommendation to a non-technical decision-maker in under five minutes."

4. The alignment table

A simple three-column table — Outcome / Assessment / Weight — instantly demonstrates curricular competence. Very few candidates include one. Anyone who has done accreditation work will notice.

Learning outcome Assessed by Weight
1 · Select and defend a method given a decision Decision memos (4), Client project brief 25%
2 · Execute analyses on real data Weekly labs, Analysis portfolio 30%
3 · Communicate to non-technical audiences Client presentation, Executive summary 30%
4 · Evaluate the limits of an analysis Critique assignment, Reflection 15%

5. Assessment scheme

Show your pedagogical values through structure. Frequent low-stakes work signals attention to skill-building. A single midterm and final signals a content-delivery model. Neither is wrong; be deliberate and be able to defend it. Include the grading scale and late policy.

6. The week-by-week schedule

This is the heart of the document and where committees look hardest for competence. A strong schedule shows:

  • Deliberate sequencing — a visible logic to the order, not just a march through textbook chapters
  • Realistic pacing — including a lighter week around midterms and a buffer week before finals
  • Reasonable reading loads — 40 pages of dense theory per week for a 100-level course is a red flag to anyone experienced
  • Named readings with real citations — placeholders like "readings TBD" undercut the whole document
  • Assessment due dates distributed rather than stacked
  • Currency — at least a few readings from the last three years
Sample — week-by-week excerpt showing motivation-before-mechanics sequencing
Wk Topic Reading / Prep Due
1 What decisions look like before data touches them Course case: "The Reformulation" (instructor-written) —
2 Measurement as a design choice Reyes (2024), JAR Practitioner Notes, pp. 44–51 Lab 1
3 Decision-first problem: two package designs, six weeks of sales data None — in-class data release Decision Memo 1
4 Why we disagreed: variation, noise, and the need for a test Textbook Ch. 4; peer memos from Wk 3 Lab 2
5 Comparing two groups: the t-test, arriving as an answer Textbook Ch. 5 Lab 3
6 Confidence, and what a p-value does not tell a VP Wasserstein & Lazar (2016), ASA statement Decision Memo 2
7 Client project launch — briefs distributed, teams formed Client brief packet Team charter

7. Policies

Attendance, late work, academic integrity, accessibility/accommodations, and — as of the current landscape — a clear and thoughtful generative AI policy. That last one is now read closely by committees as a signal of whether you're teaching in the present. Take a defensible position, whatever it is, and explain the reasoning to students.

A workable AI policy pattern

State (a) what's permitted, (b) what's prohibited, (c) what must be disclosed, and (d) why, tied to the learning outcomes. Example: "You may use generative AI to debug code, explore alternative phrasings, and check your understanding. You may not use it to produce the analytical reasoning in your decision memos, because that reasoning is the skill this course exists to build. Any AI use must be noted in a one-line appendix. I will not penalize disclosed use that follows this policy."

8. Resources and support

Tutoring, writing center, disability services, food and housing insecurity resources, mental health services. At teaching-focused institutions this section is read as evidence of student-centeredness — and, more importantly, it's the right thing to include.

Formatting and length

6–10 pages. PDF. Named Lastname_SampleSyllabus_COURSECODE.pdf. Use the target institution's course numbering, credit-hour language, and term structure (semester vs. quarter). Include a short header note if it's a proposed course: "Proposed course, prepared for [Institution]. Aligned to [Program] learning goals."

Module 6 checklist

  • Chose a required/high-need course, not only a passion elective
  • Course number, credit hours, and term structure match their conventions
  • 4–6 measurable learning outcomes using observable verbs
  • Outcome-to-assessment alignment table included
  • Full 14–15 week schedule with real, cited readings — no "TBD"
  • Pacing is realistic; assessments distributed, not stacked
  • At least some readings from the last three years
  • Explicit and reasoned generative AI policy
  • Accessibility, integrity, and student support sections present
  • 6–10 pages, PDF, clearly labeled as a sample or proposed course

Module 6bThe Supporting Documents

The four documents nobody teaches you to write, all of which are read, and two of which can sink an otherwise strong file.

Diversity, equity, and inclusion statement

Required at many institutions, prohibited at some, and increasingly variable by state and system. Check the posting and check the institution's current policy. Where it's requested, it's evaluated seriously — and it's the document where generic writing is most conspicuous.

Length: 1–2 pages. Structure that works:

  1. What you've actually done — specific actions with specific populations. Course redesigns, mentoring programs, recruitment work, accessibility improvements, curriculum revisions, pipeline programs. Include what you learned from the ones that didn't work.
  2. What you do in the classroom — concrete pedagogical practices: how you structure group work to avoid the usual dynamics, how you handle participation for students who won't speak first, what your assessment design does for students with irregular schedules or caregiving responsibilities, how you build a course that works for a student on their third attempt.
  3. What you'd do here — tied to this institution's actual student body. If it's an HSI, a rural-serving regional, a heavily transfer or first-generation campus, a community college — the specifics of that population should shape the paragraph.
The failure mode

Abstract commitment with no actions attached. "I am deeply committed to fostering an inclusive environment and value diverse perspectives" tells a committee nothing and is instantly recognizable as filler. One concrete thing you did — with what happened as a result — outweighs a page of stated commitment. If your track record here is thin, say what you've observed and what you intend to do; honesty reads better than inflation.

References and letters

  • How many: Three is standard, four to five for senior roles. Follow the posting exactly.
  • Who: A mix that covers your claims — someone who can speak to research, someone to teaching, and someone who has supervised you. For Path A: a department chair or faculty member who has seen you teach is worth more than a senior executive who hasn't.
  • Ask properly: Six to eight weeks out. Ask in a way that lets them decline: "Would you be able to write me a strong letter for teaching-focused positions?" The word "strong" gives them an honorable exit, and a lukewarm letter does more damage than a missing one.
  • Give them a packet: Your CV, the job posting, your cover letter for that job, a bulleted list of the specific things you'd like them to speak to, and the deadline in bold. This is not presumptuous; letter-writers are grateful for it and the letters get better.
  • Track and remind: A spreadsheet with institution, deadline, submission method, and status. One gentle reminder at two weeks out and one at three days out.
  • Thank them, and tell them the outcome either way. This is how you get to ask again.

Teaching portfolio

Requested at teaching-focused institutions and at the campus-visit stage nearly everywhere. Typically 15–30 pages, assembled as a single PDF:

  • Teaching philosophy (2 pages)
  • List of courses taught with enrollments and roles
  • One or two sample syllabi
  • Sample assignments and the rubrics that go with them
  • Evaluation summaries — quantitative, with departmental comparison, across all courses (not just your best)
  • Selected student comments, presented honestly
  • Peer observation letters
  • Evidence of teaching development: workshops attended, certificates, SoTL reading
  • A brief statement of teaching goals
Presenting imperfect evaluations

Never cherry-pick to the point of distortion — committees have seen it and it costs you credibility. Present the full picture with context: "My evaluations in the required 200-level course run about 0.4 below my elective courses, consistent with the departmental pattern for required quantitative courses. Here's what I changed in 2024 and here's the trend since." Owning the pattern is more persuasive than hiding it.

Web presence

Assume every committee member Googles you before the screen call. What they should find:

  • A personal site that ranks first for your name: bio, CV PDF, courses, teaching statement, contact, headshot
  • Google Scholar and ORCID current (if you publish); LinkedIn current and consistent with your CV (if you're Path A)
  • Nothing that contradicts your materials — mismatched dates and titles are the most common problem
  • No public conflict, no unprofessional content, no abandoned profiles with stale information

Module 6b checklist

  • DEI statement (if requested) grounded in specific past actions and classroom practices
  • Three to five referees confirmed, asked with the word "strong," and sent a full packet
  • Reference tracking spreadsheet built with deadlines and submission methods
  • Teaching portfolio assembled as a single PDF with full evaluation data in context
  • Personal website live and first result for your name
  • All online profiles consistent with the CV — dates and titles checked

Module 7The Teaching Demo

For teaching-focused positions, the demo decides the hire more often than any other component. Committees will forgive a mediocre research talk from a candidate who taught brilliantly. They will not hire someone who taught badly, no matter how good the paper.

What it is and when it's used

A teaching demonstration is a live class session — 20 to 50 minutes — delivered to some combination of real students, faculty, and committee members. It appears in several forms, and the form changes how you should prepare:

Format Typical length Audience What's actually being tested
Real class session — you teach an actual enrolled course 50–75 min Enrolled students + 2–4 observers Everything. Rapport, pacing, question handling, whether students engage with a stranger. The most revealing format and the most common at teaching-focused schools.
Mock class to faculty 20–30 min Faculty only, sometimes role-playing students Structure, clarity, and how you handle adversarial or deliberately naïve questions.
Mixed session 30–50 min Volunteer students + faculty The same as a real class, but with lower student stakes, so you must work harder to generate engagement.
Recorded demo — submitted with application 10–20 min Committee, asynchronously Production competence, on-camera presence, ability to hold attention with no live feedback.
Online/synchronous demo 30–45 min Committee over Zoom Whether you can teach in the modality many programs now depend on. Increasingly common and frequently under-prepared for.

When it's used: nearly always at teaching-focused institutions, liberal arts colleges, and community colleges; usually at professional schools; sometimes at R2s; occasionally at R1s where it may be replaced or supplemented by a "chalk talk" on research. Path A and Path C candidates should assume a demo is coming and prepare one before it's requested.

The three questions the committee is silently asking
  1. Would I want to sit in this class? (Engagement)
  2. Did students actually learn something in these 30 minutes? (Effectiveness)
  3. Can this person handle a question they didn't anticipate? (Robustness)

Everything in your preparation should serve those three. Notice that "did they cover a lot of material" is not on the list — and yet over-coverage is the most common failure.

How to make a good one

Step 1 — Get the brief right (do this the moment you're invited)

Email the coordinator with these questions. Asking them is itself a positive signal — it shows you think about context before content.

Thank you — I'm looking forward to it. A few questions so I can prepare something that fits your students: 1. What course and level is this, and where does the session fall in the term? 2. What have students covered immediately before this session? 3. Is there a specific topic you'd like me to teach, or is it my choice? 4. How many students, and are they enrolled students or volunteers? 5. Room setup — fixed seating or movable? Screen, whiteboard, document camera? 6. Should I plan for the full time, or leave time for committee Q&A afterward? 7. Will faculty observers participate as students or observe silently? 8. Is there anything about this student population I should know?

Step 2 — Choose the topic (if it's your choice)

Pick a topic that meets four criteria:

  • You could teach it with the lights off. Never use a demo to teach something you're still learning.
  • It contains a genuine difficulty — a common misconception, a counterintuitive result, a place where students reliably go wrong. Teaching something easy demonstrates nothing.
  • It has a visible payoff within the session. Students should end knowing something they didn't know at minute one.
  • It's core, not exotic. Teach a concept from the required curriculum, not your niche research. The committee is assessing whether you can teach their courses.

Step 3 — Design for one idea

The single most common failure in teaching demos is cramming a full lecture into 30 minutes. Committees read that as an inability to prioritize, which is the core skill of course design.

One idea. One misconception addressed. One thing students do. One visible payoff.

A 30-minute structure that reliably works
Time What happens Purpose
0:00–2:00 Hook. A concrete problem, a surprising claim, a two-option poll, a short scenario. No self-introduction beyond your name. Establishes that this session is about them, not you
2:00–4:00 Frame. "By the end of these thirty minutes you'll be able to X." Then say why X matters. Gives students something to hold onto
4:00–12:00 Build. Develop the idea. Two examples maximum. Check for understanding at least twice with a real question. The substance
12:00–20:00 Do. Students work — think-pair-share, a problem, a short case, a vote-and-defend. This segment is non-negotiable. You must move around the room. The segment that separates strong demos from adequate ones
20:00–26:00 Debrief. Take answers, including wrong ones. Make a wrong answer useful publicly. This is the highest-scoring moment available to you. Demonstrates question handling and classroom safety simultaneously
26:00–29:00 Land it. Return to the hook. Restate the one idea. Name what comes next in the imagined course. Shows you think in arcs, not sessions
29:00–30:00 Close on time. Thank them. Stop. Running over is read as a discipline problem

Step 4 — Make students do something

A demo with no student activity is a talk. Even with a room of faculty pretending to be students, build in participation — it's the thing they're watching for, and faculty observers will play along.

Activities that work in a compressed session:

  • Think-pair-share — 60 seconds alone, 90 seconds in pairs, then report out. Nearly foolproof.
  • Predict-then-reveal — pose a problem, have them commit to an answer by show of hands, then reveal. Commitment before information is what makes learning stick.
  • Sort or rank — give six items, have pairs rank them and defend the ordering.
  • Find the error — hand out a flawed analysis, argument, or design and have them locate the flaw.
  • One-minute paper — at the end: "what's still muddy?" Collecting these in front of the committee visibly demonstrates formative assessment.

Step 5 — Prepare your handling of hard moments

Moment What to do
Nobody answers your question Wait. Count to seven silently — it feels eternal and it works. If still nothing: "Turn to the person next to you and take thirty seconds." Never answer your own question in the first ten seconds; that teaches the room not to participate.
A student gives a wrong answer Find the correct instinct inside it and name it first. "You're right that we need to compare the groups — say more about why that comparison alone might mislead us." Never say "no." This moment is watched more closely than any other.
A faculty observer asks a hostile or overly advanced question Answer briefly at the level of the class, then offer to continue after. "Short answer for now is X — happy to go deeper on the edge case afterward." You're being tested on whether you protect the session's level, not on whether you know the answer.
You're asked something you don't know "I don't know. Here's how I'd find out." Then actually describe the method. This scores well. Bluffing scores catastrophically.
Technology fails Keep teaching. Have a whiteboard version of everything. A candidate who loses five minutes to a projector is remembered for that; a candidate who shrugs and picks up a marker is remembered for composure.
You're running long Cut the second example, never the student activity or the close. Decide the cut point in advance so you don't have to improvise it.
A student dominates "That's useful — let me hear from someone who hasn't spoken yet." Handled gracefully, this is a strong signal of classroom management.

Step 6 — Rehearse properly

  • Full run-throughs out loud: at least five. Silent review does not count. The demo is a performance and it needs rehearsal, not review.
  • Record yourself once and watch it. Unpleasant, unavoidable, and the fastest way to find your verbal tics and pacing problems.
  • Do one run in front of two real humans who will ask you unexpected questions.
  • Time every segment and know your cut points cold.
  • Rehearse the first ninety seconds until it's automatic. Nerves peak at the start; automaticity carries you through.

Step 7 — Logistics that get overlooked

  • Arrive 20 minutes early and test the room, the clicker, the display resolution, and your ability to be heard from the back.
  • Bring a printed handout — it works when nothing else does and it gives students something to write on. Bring five extra copies.
  • Bring your own adapters and a backup of your slides on a USB drive and in email to yourself.
  • Learn students' names if you can — even three of them, from a roster or by asking as they arrive. It is disproportionately memorable.
  • Leave a one-page handout with your name and the session's key idea. It sits on the table during deliberation.
  • Dress one notch above the department's norm.
Special guidance: the online demo

Camera at eye level, light in front of you, plain background, and a headset mic — laptop audio is the most common quality problem. Use names constantly ("Marcus, what do you think?"). Use the chat deliberately and read it aloud. Use polls or breakout rooms if you have more than eight participants, and tell them what will happen before you send them out. Look at the camera, not the faces, when you're making a point that matters. And build in more explicit participation prompts than you would in person — silence online is heavier and lasts longer.

What separates a strong demo from an adequate one

  • Students talk more than expected. Adequate demos have a few answers. Strong demos have students arguing with each other.
  • A wrong answer became the best moment of the session.
  • You covered less than you could have, deliberately, and said so.
  • The close returned to the opening. The session had a shape.
  • You used the room. Moved, wrote, sat on a desk, went to the back. Standing behind a podium reading slides is the default failure.
  • Someone learned your name and used it.

Module 7 checklist

  • Sent the logistics questions to the coordinator
  • Chose a core-curriculum topic containing a genuine difficulty
  • Designed around one idea with one visible payoff
  • Built in a mandatory student activity of 6+ minutes
  • Prepared responses for the seven hard moments
  • Five full out-loud rehearsals completed, with one recorded and watched
  • First ninety seconds memorized
  • Cut points identified in advance
  • Printed handout prepared with your name and the key idea
  • Whiteboard-only fallback version ready
  • Adapters, USB backup, and emailed slide copy

Module 8Networking

This is the module people skip, and it is the module with the highest return on effort in the entire masterclass. A very large share of academic positions — especially adjunct, lecturer, professor-of-practice, and visiting lines — are effectively shaped before they are posted. Not corruptly. Simply because when a department realizes it has a need, someone in the room says a name.

Your entire objective is to be a name someone says.

That is a lower bar and a more humane goal than "networking" usually implies. You are not trying to charm strangers into doing you favors. You are trying to become a known quantity in a small number of specific places, so that when a need arises you are already in the room's memory.

Why this works structurally

Search committees are made of overworked faculty doing an unpaid administrative task on top of a full load. Reading 200 applications is miserable. A known name is a shortcut through that misery — not because it's unfair, but because it reduces uncertainty. When your file arrives and a committee member says "I've talked to her, she's serious," your application has been pre-validated. That's the whole mechanism.

How to find people to reach out to

Build the target list first

Do not network broadly. Network narrowly and deeply into 15–25 specific institutions you would genuinely accept a job at. Geography, institution type, and program fit should all pass before an institution goes on the list. A focused list of twenty beats a scattered list of two hundred, because depth is what produces the "I've talked to her" sentence.

For each institution, build a small map:

Who Why they matter Where to find them
Department chair Knows the staffing needs before anyone. The single highest-value contact for adjunct and lecturer lines. Department website, "About" or "Faculty & Staff"
Program or area director Owns curriculum for your specific area; often the person who identifies the course gap Program pages, catalog
Faculty whose work overlaps yours Natural conversation, potential internal advocate, and often on the search committee Faculty pages, Google Scholar, recent conference programs
Faculty who teach the courses you want They know whether the course is over- or under-staffed, and whether they want to keep teaching it Course schedules — usually public
Recent hires (last 2–3 years) The most generous and most informative people you can talk to. They remember the process, they know the internal politics, and they're flattered to be asked. "News" pages, "new faculty" announcements
Director of internships/careers (professional schools) Path A's best entry point — your industry network is directly valuable to them Program staff pages
Adjunct coordinator / associate chair Often the person who actually fills last-minute course needs Department staff listings

Where to find people beyond the institution list

  • Conference programs — especially teaching-focused divisions and pedagogy tracks, which are smaller, friendlier, and full of the people who staff teaching lines. This is dramatically underused.
  • Journal editorial boards and reviewer lists in your area.
  • Your own alumni network — your degree program's graduates are the warmest possible contacts and most people never mine this. Ask your department for the alumni list.
  • Second-degree LinkedIn connections into target departments. A shared connection converts a cold message into a warm one.
  • Authors of things you actually read — the most natural reason to reach out that exists.
  • Professional associations — teaching sections, regional chapters, special interest groups. Volunteering for one of these is the highest-leverage networking act available and almost nobody does it.
  • Podcast and webinar guests in your area — already comfortable talking to strangers about their work.
Track it or it doesn't happen

One spreadsheet: Name · Institution · Role · How found · Date contacted · Response · Date of call · Notes · Follow-up due · Last touch. Twenty rows is a real network. The follow-up column is the one that matters — most people's networking fails at the second contact, not the first.

What to message them

Cold outreach fails for predictable reasons: it's long, it's vague, it asks for something expensive, and it's obviously a template. Fix those four things and your response rate goes from near-zero to somewhere between 30% and 60%.

The five rules of academic cold outreach
  1. Under 150 words. Every additional sentence reduces your response rate.
  2. Prove you did the work. One specific, accurate reference to their work, course, or program — not the institution's reputation.
  3. Ask for something small and bounded. Fifteen or twenty minutes. Name the number.
  4. Never ask for a job. Ask for perspective. The job conversation happens later, or it happens on their initiative, which is far better.
  5. Make declining easy. "No problem at all if the timing doesn't work" removes the social cost of ignoring you, which paradoxically increases replies.

Script 1 — Cold outreach to a faculty member whose work you know

Subject: Question about your work on [SPECIFIC TOPIC] Dear Dr. [LAST NAME], I'm [ONE-LINE IDENTITY: e.g., "a PhD candidate in sociology at Michigan" / "a consumer insights leader at Halberd Brands who has been teaching marketing analytics at St. Edward's since 2021"]. I read your [PAPER/CHAPTER/ARTICLE] on [TOPIC] and it reframed something I've been working on — specifically, [ONE SENTENCE ON THE ACTUAL CONNECTION]. I'm beginning to think seriously about moving toward a faculty role, and I'd value fifteen minutes of your perspective on how someone with my background should be thinking about it. I'm not asking about openings — I'd just like to learn from someone doing the work. Completely understand if the timing doesn't work. Best, [NAME] [LINK TO YOUR SITE]

Script 2 — Outreach to a department chair (highest value for Paths A and C)

Subject: Teaching interest — [YOUR AREA] Dear Dr. [LAST NAME], I'm [NAME], [ROLE] at [ORGANIZATION], where I've spent [N] years in [AREA]. I've also taught [COURSE] at [INSTITUTION] for the past [N] years. I noticed your program requires [SPECIFIC COURSE OR SEQUENCE] — that's the exact area I work in and teach in, and I'd be glad to be a resource to the department, whether that's guest lecturing, supplying a capstone project brief, or covering a section if you ever have a gap. Would you have twenty minutes in the next few weeks? I'd like to understand your program's needs better, with no expectation on your end. Best, [NAME]
Why this one works

It offers before it asks. A chair reading this sees a low-risk, zero-cost resource — a guest lecturer, a project brief, a backup for a staffing emergency. Chairs collect those people. And the person a chair calls in August when a section suddenly needs covering is the person who once offered.

Script 3 — Warm intro request to a mutual connection

Subject: Quick intro ask — [TARGET NAME] at [INSTITUTION] Hi [NAME], Hope you're well. [ONE SENTENCE OF GENUINE PERSONAL CONNECTION — not filler] I noticed you know [TARGET] at [INSTITUTION]. I'm exploring a move toward teaching in [AREA] and their program is one of the few doing [SPECIFIC THING]. Would you be comfortable making an introduction? I've written a short blurb below you can forward or ignore entirely — and no worries at all if you'd rather not. [3-SENTENCE FORWARDABLE BLURB: who you are, what you teach, what you're asking for] Thanks either way, [NAME]

The forwardable blurb is the key detail. It makes saying yes a ten-second act instead of a writing task, which is why this version gets forwarded and the version without it doesn't.

Script 4 — After meeting someone at a conference

Subject: Great to meet you at [CONFERENCE] Hi [NAME], Really enjoyed our conversation about [SPECIFIC THING YOU DISCUSSED] — [ONE SPECIFIC THING THEY SAID THAT YOU'VE THOUGHT ABOUT SINCE]. You mentioned [RESOURCE/PAPER/PERSON]; here's [SOMETHING RELEVANT YOU CAN OFFER IN RETURN] in case it's useful. Would be glad to keep the conversation going if you're ever up for a call. [NAME]

Send this within 48 hours. The half-life of a conference conversation is about four days.

Script 5 — Reaching out to a recent hire

Subject: Question from someone going through what you just went through Dear Dr. [LAST NAME], Congratulations on joining [INSTITUTION] — I saw the announcement. I'm [ONE-LINE IDENTITY], and I'm preparing to go on the market for [TYPE OF ROLE]. You've just been through the process I'm about to enter, at exactly the kind of institution I'm targeting. Would you have fifteen minutes to tell me what surprised you? Happy to work around whatever's left of your schedule. Best, [NAME]

Recent hires reply at a much higher rate than anyone else, they give you the most useful information available, and they're often on the next search committee. Contact every recent hire at every institution on your list.

Script 6 — The re-engagement (for contacts that went quiet)

Subject: [SPECIFIC THING THEY'D CARE ABOUT] Hi [NAME], We spoke back in [MONTH] about [TOPIC] — your point about [THEIR POINT] has stuck with me. I wrote something on it: [LINK]. Thought of you. Also, small update: [ONE LINE — new course, new publication, formally on the market this fall]. Hope the term's going well. [NAME]
Email hygiene
  • Send Tuesday–Thursday, 7–9am their time. Faculty triage email in the morning.
  • Never send during finals week, the first week of term, or late August.
  • One follow-up only, ten to fourteen days later, three sentences: "Following up briefly in case this got buried. Still glad to talk if useful — and no problem if not." Then stop. Two follow-ups is persistent; three is a reputation.
  • Use your professional email with a real signature and your website link.
  • No attachments on first contact. Link to your CV instead; attachments to strangers get filtered.
  • Get the title right. "Dr." unless you know otherwise. Getting this wrong is a small error that lands badly.

How to run coffee chats and continue the conversation

Before the call (20 minutes of prep)

  • Read two things they wrote. Not skimmed — read.
  • Look at their courses and their department's curriculum.
  • Write down five questions. Not a script — a safety net for when the conversation stalls.
  • Decide the one thing you want them to remember about you. Usually your teachable zone statement.
  • Decide what you can offer. Always have something.

The 20-minute structure

Minutes What happens
0–2 Thanks, and a specific reason you wanted to talk to them. Then a 30-second version of who you are — practiced, not improvised.
2–12 They talk. Ask your questions. Listen more than you speak. This is the part people get wrong by treating the call as a pitch.
12–17 You share your situation and what you're aiming for. This is where the teachable zone statement goes, once, cleanly.
17–19 Offer something. Then ask the referral question.
19–20 Thank them, confirm any follow-up, end on time or early. Ending early is remembered fondly.

Questions that actually produce useful information

  • "What's the department trying to build over the next few years?"
  • "Which courses are hardest to staff?" (The single most valuable question you can ask. Ask it every time.)
  • "What kind of person does well in your department, and what kind struggles?"
  • "If you were advising someone with my background, what would you tell them to do in the next six months?"
  • "What surprised you about how searches actually run here?"
  • "What do candidates from industry usually get wrong when they apply to programs like yours?" (Path A)
  • "How does your department think about the difference between adjunct and full-time hires?" (Path C)
  • "Who else should I be talking to?" (Always. Every call.)
The referral question, phrased so it works

Not: "Do you know anyone hiring?" — which asks them to solve your problem.

Instead: "Is there one other person you'd suggest I talk to?" — bounded, easy, and it's how a list of twenty becomes a network of sixty. Ask it at the end of every single conversation. Roughly half the time you'll get a name, and a name from a colleague converts at a far higher rate than any cold email.

What to offer (you must have something)

Reciprocity is what converts a one-time call into a relationship. You have more to offer than you think:

  • Guest lecture. Free, useful, and it puts you physically in front of a department. The highest-conversion offer in this entire module.
  • A capstone or project brief from your organization. Programs are chronically short of real client projects. (Path A — this is enormously valuable to them.)
  • Industry contacts for their students' internships or job placement.
  • A dataset for teaching use.
  • A panel appearance or a career-night speaker slot.
  • A relevant article, person, or resource — the smallest offer, always available.
  • Reading a draft or reviewing for a journal they edit.

The guest lecture pipeline

This is the most reliable path from outsider to insider, and it is available to almost everyone: (1) offer a guest lecture in your area; (2) deliver an excellent one — treat it like a teaching demo, because it is one; (3) meet two or three other faculty while you're on campus; (4) follow up with the chair and offer to do it again; (5) become the person the department calls when a section needs covering; (6) be inside the building when a line opens. Many adjunct and lecturer positions are filled through exactly this sequence, and it typically takes six to eighteen months.

Mindset

Most people don't fail at networking because they lack scripts. They fail because something in them resists doing it. Here is the honest version of the objections.

"This feels transactional and gross."

It feels that way when you're extracting. It stops feeling that way when you're contributing. Every framework in this module is built on offering first — a lecture, a brief, a contact, a resource. If you spend a year being useful to twenty departments, you have not manipulated anyone; you have participated in a field. The discomfort is a signal to reorient toward giving, not a signal to stop.

"I'm imposing on busy people."

You are asking for fifteen minutes with an explicit permission to decline. Faculty are asked for far more, far less politely, constantly. And the specific ask you're making — "tell me about your work and your field" — is one most academics genuinely enjoy. The people who are too busy will not reply, and that is a complete and painless outcome.

"I don't have anything to offer someone senior to me."

Usually false, and it's the belief that does the most damage. If you're Path A, you have a professional network, real data, live cases, and student placement pathways — all of which departments actively want and rarely have. If you're Path B, you have current methods knowledge, energy for collaboration, and a reason for a senior person to feel useful, which is its own reward. If you're Path C, you have front-line knowledge of how the curriculum actually lands on students. Everyone has something. Name yours before your next call.

"I've been rejected and I don't want to reach out again."

Rejection in academic hiring is overwhelmingly about fit, timing, and internal candidates — not about your worth. A department that ranks you second this year is a department that knows your name next year, and second-place candidates are called with startling frequency when a first choice declines or a new line opens. Send the gracious note. Stay in the network. That note costs three minutes and it has landed people jobs eighteen months later.

"I don't have time."

Two hours a week. One hour of outreach — that's four to six messages — and one hour of calls and follow-up. Over six months that's roughly 100 messages and 40 conversations, which is a substantial network in any field. Compare that to the time spent polishing a cover letter that goes into a portal with 240 others.

The reframe that makes this sustainable

Stop thinking of this as a job search and start thinking of it as joining a field. You are going to be in this world for decades. The conversations you have this year with people whose work you admire are not instrumental steps toward a hire — they are the beginning of a professional life. If you get a job out of it, good. If you get twenty colleagues out of it, you have something that outlasts any single search. That framing is both more accurate and, conveniently, far more effective.

Module 9The Interview

By the time you're interviewing, everyone in the room is qualified. The committee has already decided you can do the job on paper. What they're deciding now is whether they want you in the building for the next twenty years — and whether, if they offer, you'll accept.

What to expect

Stage 1 — The screening interview (20–45 minutes, video)

Usually the full search committee, often with a rigid script — the same questions asked of every candidate in the same order, because HR requires it. That rigidity is useful information: it means your answers are being compared directly, and it means unusual answers stand out.

  • Purpose: narrow eight to twelve candidates down to three or four.
  • What decides it: clarity, fit, and whether you sound like a colleague. Almost nobody wins a job at this stage; plenty of people lose one.
  • Prepare: a 90-second self-introduction, a 3-minute research summary, a 2-minute teaching summary, three specific reasons for this institution, and three questions to ask.
  • Setup matters: camera at eye level, light in front, plain background, wired headset, and a printed one-page cheat sheet taped just beside the camera so your eyes stay in frame.
The screen-call trap

Answering at length. On video, a three-minute answer feels like eight. Aim for 90 seconds to two minutes per answer, then stop and let them follow up. Candidates who leave room for follow-up questions are experienced as conversational; candidates who fill every silence are experienced as exhausting.

Stage 2 — The campus visit (1–2 days)

There is no off-duty moment

The drive from the airport, the walk between buildings, the dinner, the "informal" coffee with a grad student — all of it is reported. The most common way strong candidates lose offers is a careless comment: dismissiveness about the location, a joke about the teaching load, criticism of a current employer, or condescension toward a staff member. Assume everything gets back to the committee, because it does.

Stamina and logistics

  • Eat when food is offered. You will not get another chance and you will be talking for nine hours.
  • Ask for the schedule in advance, plus dietary restrictions and accessibility needs. Asking is normal.
  • Build in bathroom breaks — say "could I have five minutes?" between blocks. Everyone does this.
  • Bring printed copies of your CV, syllabus, and one-page research summary.
  • Keep a small notebook. Write down names immediately. Using someone's name on day two is disproportionately effective.
  • Keep alcohol to zero or one drink at dinner, regardless of what others do.
  • Ask every group the same one or two questions — the variation in answers tells you more about the department than anything on the website.

The research talk (job talk)

Typically 40–50 minutes plus 10–20 of questions. At R1s this is the single most decisive component. At teaching-focused schools it may be shorter or replaced by a teaching demo.

Structure

Minutes Content
0–3 The question and why it matters. Accessible to everyone in the room including first-year grad students and faculty from other subfields. If you lose them here, you never get them back.
3–8 The gap. What's known, what isn't, and why the gap has persisted. Be generous to prior work — the person you're dismissing may be in the room or may be their friend.
8–15 Your approach. Method, data, design. Enough detail to be credible, not enough to lose the non-specialists.

Slide craft

  • Roughly one slide per 60–90 seconds. Forty-minute talk, 25–35 slides.
  • One idea per slide. A sentence-form title that states the takeaway — not "Results," but "Procedural legitimacy predicted allocation better than evidence quality."
  • No slide you'd have to apologize for ("I know this is busy…"). If you'd apologize, cut it.
  • Figures readable from the back of a large room. Test this literally.
  • Have three backup slides for anticipated technical questions. Pulling one up when asked is impressive.

Handling questions

  • Repeat the question before answering. Buys thinking time and helps the room hear it.
  • Answer the question asked, not the one you prepared for.
  • "I don't know" is a strong answer when followed by how you'd find out.
  • Concede real limitations. Defending an indefensible point is the fastest way to look junior. "You're right, that's a genuine limitation — here's why I made the trade-off" is what a colleague sounds like.
  • If someone is hostile, stay warm and brief. The room is watching your temperament, not the substance. Often the hostile questioner is testing exactly that.
  • If a question is confused, find the reasonable version of it and answer that.

The lecture demo within the visit

Everything in Module 7 applies. Three visit-specific additions:

  • You will be tired. The demo is often on day two after eleven hours of talking. Rehearse to the point where you could deliver it exhausted, because you will be.
  • Real enrolled students may be indifferent — they didn't choose to have a stranger teach their class. Win them in the first two minutes with something concrete, and use their names.
  • Ask what they've just covered and reference it explicitly in the first minute. "You worked on X last week — today we're going to break X." Nothing signals preparation faster.

The question bank

Prepare an answer for every question below. Not memorized — but rehearsed enough that you're not composing under pressure. For each, the note in italics tells you what's actually being assessed.

Opening and identity

  • "Tell us about yourself." Whether you can prioritize. 90 seconds: current role, through-line, why this position, stop.
  • "Why this position, and why now?" Whether you'll accept an offer. Be specific about them.
  • "Why our institution specifically?" The fit test. Name courses, programs, people, student population. Generic answers are fatal here.
  • "Where do you see yourself in five years?" Whether you'd stay. Describe a trajectory that requires this institution.
  • "What questions do you have for us?" Seriousness. Never have none.

Teaching

  • "Describe your teaching philosophy." Give the claim from Module 4, then one concrete practice. Don't recite the document.
  • "What courses could you teach for us?" Your three-tier list. Include the required ones by name.
  • "How would you teach [their required course]?" Have a real answer. This question is asked constantly and answered vaguely.
  • "Describe a time a class went badly. What did you change?" Reflective practice. The candidate who has no example is the concerning one.
  • "How do you handle students who are underprepared?" Mechanisms, not sympathy.
  • "How do you engage students who don't participate?" Specific techniques — structured pairs, cold-call-with-warning, written-first formats.
  • "How do you approach generative AI in your courses?" Now near-universal. Have a considered position tied to learning outcomes.
  • "What would you do about a student who challenges your expertise?" Temperament. Curiosity, not defensiveness.
  • "How do you assess learning?" Alignment thinking. Reference outcomes.
  • "What's a new course you'd want to develop?" Curricular imagination — but tie it to a program need, not just your interest.

Research

  • "Summarize your research for a non-specialist." Practice this until it's 60 seconds and jargon-free.
  • "What's your next project?" Status markers. Data, IRB, drafts, dates.
  • "What's your funding plan?" Name programs and cycles.
  • "How would you involve students in your research?" Specific tasks by level.
  • "How will you maintain research productivity on a 3-3 load?" Realism. A concrete weekly structure beats an expression of commitment.
  • "What would you publish in the next three years?" Calibration. Name plausible venues.
  • "How does your work relate to Professor X's?" Whether you did the reading.

Service, collegiality, and the department

  • "What service would you want to take on?" Willingness. Name something specific and unglamorous.
  • "Describe a conflict with a colleague and how you handled it." The collegiality test. Never blame. Show what you learned.
  • "How do you contribute to an inclusive environment?" Actions with outcomes, not values.
  • "What would you want to change about our program?" A trap and an opportunity. Answer with humility plus one genuine, well-informed observation. Pure flattery reads as unserious; sweeping critique reads as arrogant.
  • "How do you work with colleagues who disagree with you?"

  • To students: "What do you wish this program had?" · "What makes a professor good here?" · "What do students complain about?"

Salary, benefits, or startup specifics before an offer — that conversation belongs to negotiation. The offer and negotiation

Between the visit and the offer

  • Thank-you emails within 24 hours to the chair, the search chair, and anyone who spent substantial time with you. Reference something specific from each conversation. Individual notes, not a group email.
  • If you have a competing timeline, tell the chair honestly and early. Departments can sometimes accelerate; they can never accelerate a deadline you told them about the day before it hits.
  • Silence is normal. Searches take weeks. One polite status inquiry after the stated timeline has passed is appropriate.

When the offer comes

  • Express genuine enthusiasm, then ask for it in writing and for time to review. Two weeks is standard and reasonable to request.
  • Never accept on the phone, and never negotiate on the first call. "I'm delighted. Could you send the written details? I'd like to review carefully and come back to you within the week."

Ask for everything in one round, framed as what you need to succeed rather than what you deserve. Module 10Being a Great Professor

Getting the job is the shorter half. This module is about the part nobody trains you for — and the part that determines renewal, promotion, and whether the work is sustainable.

Managing a classroom

Classroom management is not discipline. It's design. Nearly every problem that shows up in week ten was created by an ambiguity in week one. The professors who seem to have effortless classrooms are usually the ones who front-loaded the structure.

The first day sets everything

Students form a durable model of your course in the first fifty minutes. Spend that time deliberately:

  • Teach something real on day one. Do not just read the syllabus. Twenty minutes of actual content establishes that class time is used, and it is the single highest-leverage decision in the term.
  • Name the norms explicitly and say why. "I cold-call, and here's why: it makes me responsible for hearing from everyone, not just the confident. I'll always give you a moment to think first."
  • Say what you're strict about and what you're not. Ambiguity is what generates conflict. Being clearly strict about two things and clearly relaxed about others is easier to live with than being vaguely strict about everything.
  • Learn names fast. Name tents, a seating chart, photos, whatever it takes. Nothing else changes the atmosphere of a room this much for this little effort.
  • Explain the arc. Where the course goes and why it's ordered that way. Students who understand the shape complain less about the middle.

Structures that prevent problems

Structure What it prevents
Written policies for late work, missed exams, and regrades — applied without exception The single largest source of end-of-term conflict, and of accusations of unfairness
Rubrics distributed with the assignment Grade disputes. A student who saw the rubric in advance has far less standing to argue.
Low-stakes work early in the term Students discovering in week eleven that they don't understand week three
A stated response time for email (e.g., 24 hours on weekdays) Availability creep and weekend anxiety on both sides
Structured group work: assigned roles, peer evaluation, a team charter The free-rider problem, which is the most common student complaint about group projects

The week-7 feedback move

Three questions, anonymous, five minutes of class: What's helping you learn? What's getting in the way? What's one thing I could change? Then — this is the essential part — report back the following session: here's what you said, here's what I'm changing, here's what I'm not changing and why. Students who see their feedback acted on rate the course substantially higher, and you get to fix problems while there's still time. It costs ten minutes and it is the highest-return classroom practice in this module.

Managing energy and attention

  • Change the mode every 12–18 minutes. Lecture, then problem, then discussion, then lecture. Attention is a resource with a refresh cycle.
  • Use silence. After asking a question, count to seven. It feels unbearable and it produces answers.
  • Move around the room. Physical proximity does more for engagement than any slide.
  • Start on time, end on time. Every time. It's a trust signal.
  • Give the room a break in a 75-minute-plus session. You lose five minutes and get thirty back.

Dealing with difficulty

The disengaged student

Disengagement is usually a signal, not a character trait — often confusion, competing obligations, or a belief they can't succeed. The move is private and early: a short, non-accusatory email. "I noticed you've missed the last two sessions. No judgment — I wanted to check whether everything's okay and whether there's something I can do." A surprising share of these emails resolve the problem entirely.

The dominating student

Handle it structurally rather than personally. "Let's hear from someone who hasn't spoken yet." Use think-pair-share so airtime is distributed by design. If it persists, a private conversation framed around their strength: "You're clearly engaged — I want to use that. Can you help me draw out the quieter students?" That framing converts a problem into an ally more often than confrontation does.

The student who challenges your authority

Especially common for early-career faculty, women, faculty of color, and practitioners without a PhD. Two principles: never get defensive, and never over-explain your credentials. Treat the challenge as an intellectual question, engage the substance briefly, and move on. "That's a fair challenge — here's the evidence I'm relying on, and here's where it's contested. Let's look at it." Calm engagement resolves this far faster than assertion of authority, and the rest of the room reads it as confidence.

The grade dispute

  • Have a written regrade policy: requests in writing, within one week, explaining which rubric criterion was misapplied. Requiring a written argument filters out most disputes and improves the remaining ones.
  • Never regrade in the moment or in the hallway. "Send me that in writing and I'll look at it carefully."
  • Regrade the whole assignment, and say so in advance — the grade can move in either direction.
  • Document everything. If it escalates, the paper trail is what protects you.
  • Know when to involve the chair. If a student threatens, invokes a parent, or raises a formal complaint, loop in your chair immediately. Do not manage it alone.

Academic integrity

  • Follow your institution's process exactly. Do not handle it informally, however tempting — informal handling leaves you unprotected and is unfair to students handled formally.
  • Design out the opportunity: rotating problem sets, process-based assignments, drafts and check-ins, in-class components.
  • On AI specifically: detection tools are unreliable and produce false positives that fall hardest on non-native English speakers. Build assessments that make AI use either irrelevant or visible — oral defenses, in-class writing, personalized data, staged drafts — rather than relying on detection.
  • When you do confront it, be procedural and unemotional. State what you observed, cite the policy, describe the process.

Students in distress

You will encounter students in genuine crisis — mental health, food or housing insecurity, family emergency, harassment. Your role is to be a bridge, not a counselor.

  • Know your campus resources before you need them: counseling, dean of students, CARE/behavioral intervention team, food pantry, Title IX. Have the numbers saved.
  • Say plainly: "I want to make sure you're supported. Can I connect you with someone?" Then actually make the connection — walking a student to an office is dramatically more effective than giving them a URL.
  • Understand your reporting obligations. Most faculty are mandatory reporters for certain disclosures; know what yours are before a student discloses, so you can tell them at the start of the conversation.
  • Be flexible on deadlines where you can, but don't become the student's support system. That's not sustainable for you and it isn't the help they need.

Difficult colleagues and department politics

  • Say little in your first year. Listen, observe who aligns with whom, and don't take a side in a conflict you don't understand.
  • Never criticize a colleague to a student. It travels, always, and it costs you more than it costs them.
  • Find one honest senior mentor outside your immediate area, and one peer at your level. The second is often more useful.
  • Say yes to service that is visible and bounded; decline service that is invisible and unbounded. Junior faculty — and especially junior women and faculty of color — get disproportionately loaded with the second kind. Learn the phrase: "I'd like to, but I need to check with my chair about my service load."
  • Document your contributions continuously. Reconstructing them for a review file two years later is miserable and inaccurate.

Course evaluations

Course evaluations are a flawed instrument that measures real things imperfectly and is used consequentially anyway. The mature position is neither to dismiss them nor to be governed by them.

What the evidence says

  • Evaluations correlate weakly, at best, with actual learning outcomes.
  • They carry well-documented biases related to gender, race, accent, age, and appearance. Women and faculty of color receive systematically lower ratings for equivalent teaching.
  • Required, quantitative, and early-morning courses score lower than electives, regardless of instructor.
  • Grading leniency correlates with higher ratings.
  • They do reliably measure some genuinely useful things: clarity of expectations, organization, responsiveness, perceived fairness.

Knowing this lets you do two things: improve on the dimensions evaluations actually measure well, and provide context when your numbers are read by others.

Practice Why it moves the number
Mid-semester feedback with a visible response Largest single effect available. Students rate courses higher when they believe they were heard.

What not to do

Do not inflate grades, reduce rigor, or lighten the workload to chase ratings. It works in the short term, it's visible to colleagues over time, and it damages the students and the reputation you're building. Evaluations are a constraint to manage, not an objective to maximize.

How to read them without being wrecked by them

  • Don't read them the day they're released. Wait a week. The emotional response distorts your judgment about what's actionable.
  • Read all of them at once and sort: actionable, contradictory, and personal. Act on the first bucket. Note the second. Discard the third — cruel comments about your appearance or accent contain no information about your teaching.
  • Look for patterns across sections and terms, not individual comments. One student's complaint is noise; the same complaint from nine students is data.
  • Weigh comparison, not raw score. A 4.1 in a required quantitative course may be considerably stronger than a 4.6 in a senior elective.
  • Ask a trusted colleague to read them with you the first few times. They will see the pattern you're too close to see, and they'll tell you which comments to ignore.

Presenting evaluations in a review or application file

  • Give the full record with departmental comparison means, not a curated selection.
  • Contextualize honestly: course type, level, required vs. elective, enrollment, response rate.
  • Show the trend. Improvement over time is more persuasive than a single high score.
  • Pair quantitative data with peer observation letters and evidence of pedagogical development. A file resting only on student ratings is a weak file.
  • If there's a bad term, address it in one sentence with what you changed. Explained is better than hidden — and hidden things get noticed.

Building the record from day one

Whatever your institution's review process, it will ask you to document things you cannot reconstruct later. Start a folder in week one and put things in it as they happen:

  • Every syllabus, every term
  • Every evaluation, quantitative and qualitative, with departmental comparisons
  • Peer observation letters — request one per year even if not required
  • Thank-you emails from students (save them; they're evidence and they're morale)
  • Records of course and curriculum development
  • Student supervision: names, projects, outcomes, placements
  • Service: committee, dates, what you actually did
  • Publications, presentations, and submissions with dates
  • Grant submissions including unfunded ones, with scores
  • Teaching development: workshops, certificates, reading
  • A running one-page "what I did this year" file, updated monthly
The habit that makes the rest of it work

Thirty minutes on the last Friday of every month. Update the CV, drop artifacts into the record folder, add one line to the annual summary. Over six years, three hours a year of maintenance produces a complete, accurate promotion file. The alternative — reconstructing six years under deadline — is one of the more demoralizing experiences in academic life, and it produces a worse file.
